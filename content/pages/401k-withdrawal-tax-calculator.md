# Tax on 401(k) Withdrawal Calculator: 2026 Federal Estimate

This tax on 401k withdrawal calculator estimates how much of a traditional 401(k) withdrawal could go to federal income tax in 2026, whether the 10% additional tax on early distributions applies, and how much you could keep. It also shows the 20% federal withholding a plan generally takes when it pays a distribution to you.

## How the Calculator Estimates the Tax

A withdrawal from a traditional, pre-tax 401(k) is generally taxed as ordinary income. The calculator stacks the withdrawal on top of the taxable income you enter and measures the difference:

**Federal tax on the withdrawal = Tax with the withdrawal − Tax without it**

It uses the 2026 federal income tax brackets in [IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-45_IRB) for your filing status. Enter taxable income after deductions. For reference, the 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for joint filers, and $24,150 for heads of household.

For example, a single filer with $50,000 of other taxable income who withdraws $20,000 at age 50 would have $400 taxed at 12% and $19,600 at 22%, about $4,360 of federal income tax. Without an exception, the 10% additional tax adds $2,000, for a total of about $6,360, or 31.8% of the withdrawal. These are Fin Loom calculations from the 2026 brackets.

## The 10% Additional Tax on Early Withdrawals

[IRS Topic 558](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558) explains that the law imposes a 10% additional tax on certain early distributions from retirement plans, and that early distributions are generally those received before age 59½. The tax is on top of regular income tax.

Several exceptions exist. One that often applies to 401(k) plans covers distributions made after you separate from service with your employer after attaining age 55. The calculator lets you mark that an exception applies, but it does not check whether you qualify, so confirm the rule against the IRS guidance or with a tax professional.

## Withholding Is Not the Same as the Final Tax

IRS Topic 413 notes that a taxable eligible rollover distribution paid to you from an employer plan is subject to mandatory income tax withholding, generally at 20%, even if you plan to roll it over later. That amount is a prepayment. Your actual tax is worked out on your return, so the withholding can be more or less than what you finally owe, which is why the calculator shows both.

## What the Calculator Leaves Out

The estimate covers federal income tax, the 10% additional tax, and an optional flat state rate you enter. It does not model Roth 401(k) withdrawals, after-tax contributions, rollovers, required minimum distributions, credits, or how extra income can affect other parts of a return. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Is a 401(k) Withdrawal Taxed?

A traditional 401(k) withdrawal is generally taxed as ordinary income in the year you take it, at your federal bracket rates, plus any state income tax that applies.

### When Does the 10% Additional Tax Apply?

It generally applies to distributions taken before age 59½, unless an exception applies, such as separation from service after age 55 for employer plan distributions.

### Why Was 20% Withheld From My Withdrawal?

Plans generally must withhold 20% of a taxable eligible rollover distribution paid to you. It is a prepayment toward your tax, not the final amount owed.
