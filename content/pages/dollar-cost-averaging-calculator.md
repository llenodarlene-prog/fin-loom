# Dollar-Cost Averaging Calculator: Average Cost and Lump Sum Compared

This dollar-cost averaging calculator shows what happens when you invest the same dollar amount at regular intervals while the price moves. Enter how much you invest each time, how many purchases you make, and a simple price path, and it returns your average cost per share, the ending value and how that compares with investing the full amount at the first price.

```calculator
dca
```

## How the Dollar-Cost Averaging Calculator Works

[FINRA describes dollar-cost averaging](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/dollar-cost-averaging) as investing equal portions at regular intervals, regardless of market conditions. Because each purchase is the same dollar amount, you buy more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high.

The calculator builds a hypothetical price path: the price falls in a straight line to its low at the middle purchase, then moves in a straight line to the ending price. Each purchase buys amount ÷ price shares. Your average cost is the total invested ÷ total shares.

With the defaults, $500 a month for 12 months, a $100 starting price, a 20% dip and a $100 ending price, you buy about 66.38 shares for $6,000. That is an average cost of about $90.39 a share, and the shares are worth about $6,638 at $100. Investing the full $6,000 at $100 would buy 60 shares worth $6,000. These are Fin Loom calculations from a made-up price path.

## When Dollar-Cost Averaging Helps and When It Does Not

Dollar-cost averaging tends to look better when prices dip and recover during the buying period, as in the default example. It tends to look worse when prices rise steadily, because later purchases happen at higher prices than a lump sum would have paid. Set the dip to 0% and the ending price above the start to see that case.

FINRA notes that, compared with investing everything at once, spreading purchases can limit losses during a large decline, because money not yet invested is unaffected. It does not prevent losses on money already invested.

## Average Cost Versus Average Price

The calculator shows two averages. The average price is the simple average of the prices you paid. The average cost per share is total dollars invested ÷ total shares. Because equal dollar amounts buy more shares at low prices, the average cost is usually a little below the average price whenever prices vary.

## What the Calculator Leaves Out

The price path is a simple illustration, not a forecast. Real prices move unpredictably. Fees, taxes, dividends and any interest earned on cash waiting to be invested are not included. FinLoom's [buy the dip statistics](/investing/buy-the-dip-statistics/) explain why timing purchases around declines is harder than charts make it look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is Dollar-Cost Averaging?

It means investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals regardless of price. FINRA describes it as investing equal portions at regular intervals, regardless of market conditions.

### Is Dollar-Cost Averaging Better Than a Lump Sum?

Neither wins every time. Spreading purchases helps when prices dip during the buying period and hurts when prices rise steadily. The calculator lets you test both cases.

### Why Is My Average Cost Lower Than the Average Price?

Equal dollar amounts buy more shares when prices are low. Those cheaper shares pull the average cost per share below the simple average of the prices.
