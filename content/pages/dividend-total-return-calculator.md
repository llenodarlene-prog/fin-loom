# Dividend Total Return Calculator: Price Change Plus Dividends

This dividend total return calculator adds the dividends a stock paid to the change in its share price, then subtracts fees. It answers one question: how much did the whole holding earn, not just the price? Enter what you paid, what the shares are worth now or sold for, how long you held them, and the dividends per share you received.

```calculator
dividend-return
```

## How the Calculator Works

The calculator uses four formulas:

- **Price Gain:** (Current price − Purchase price) × Shares.
- **Dividend Income:** Total dividends per share × Shares.
- **Total Return:** Price gain + Dividend income − Fees.
- **Annualized Return:** (Ending value ÷ Starting value)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1.

The starting value is the purchase cost plus fees, so the percentages reflect the full amount paid out. The ending value is the shares at the current price plus the dividends received.

The default example is a Fin Loom illustration. Buying 100 shares at $50 with $10 of fees, holding them for three years while they pay $4.50 per share in dividends, and valuing them at $60 gives a $1,000 price gain and $450 of dividends. That is a total return of $1,440, or about 28.7% of the $5,010 paid. Spread evenly over three years, that equals about 8.8% a year.

## Why Total Return Is the Fuller Measure

A share price chart leaves out dividends. For income stocks, dividends can be a large part of what an investor actually earned, and a stock whose price went nowhere may still have produced a positive return. Looking at price alone can make a dividend payer look weaker than it was, and a high yield alone can hide a falling share price.

Total return combines both, so two holdings can be compared on the same basis. The bar breakdown under the results shows how much came from the price change, how much from dividends, and how much fees took away.

## Taking Dividends as Cash vs Reinvesting

By default, dividends are counted as cash received. If you switch on reinvestment, the calculator assumes the dividends bought more shares at the average of the purchase and current price, and values those extra shares at the current price. That is a simplification: a real reinvestment plan buys shares on each payment date at that day's price.

To model reinvestment payment by payment over many years, with dividend growth and share-price assumptions, use the [dividend reinvestment plan calculator](/investing/dividend-reinvestment-plan-calculator/).

## What the Result Leaves Out

The calculator does not include taxes. In a taxable account, dividends and realized gains can be taxable, and some dividends may qualify for lower federal rates; the rules are explained in [qualified dividends](/investing/qualified-dividends/). It also does not account for inflation, currency changes, or when during the holding period each dividend was paid.

A past total return describes what happened under the inputs entered. It does not predict what a stock will return next.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is Dividend Total Return?

It is the change in a stock's price plus the dividends it paid over a holding period, minus costs, shown in dollars and as a percentage of what was paid.

### How Is Annualized Return Calculated?

The calculator divides the ending value by the starting value, raises the result to the power of one divided by the years held, and subtracts one.

### Do I Enter Annual or Total Dividends?

Enter the total dividends per share received across the whole holding period. For example, three years of $1.50 annual dividends is $4.50 per share.
