# Dividend Yield Calculator: Yield and Annual Income

This dividend yield calculator turns a stock's dividend and price into a yield. Enter the share price, the dividend paid each time and how many payments a year, and it returns the annual dividend per share, the yield, and the income an investment of your chosen size would produce at that rate.

```calculator
dividend-yield
```

## How the Dividend Yield Calculator Works

Dividend yield equals annual dividends per share divided by the share price. The annual dividend is the payment per period times the number of payments a year. With the defaults, $0.50 paid quarterly is $2.00 a year; at a $50 share price, that is a 4.00% yield, and $10,000 invested would earn about $400 a year. These are Fin Loom calculations from the inputs shown.

## Why Yield Moves With Price

Because price is in the denominator, the yield rises when the price falls and drops when the price rises, even if the dividend stays the same. The table in the calculator shows the yield at prices 10% and 20% above and below today's. A very high yield can reflect a falling share price, sometimes because investors expect a dividend cut.

FinLoom's guide to [dividend vs dividend yield](/investing/dividend-vs-dividend-yield/) explains what each number tells investors.

## What the Calculator Leaves Out

Dividends are not guaranteed and can be reduced or stopped. Special one-time dividends, taxes, fees and reinvestment are not included. To model reinvested dividends, use the [dividend reinvestment plan calculator](/investing/dividend-reinvestment-plan-calculator/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Do You Calculate Dividend Yield?

Divide the annual dividend per share by the share price, then multiply by 100. A $2 annual dividend on a $50 stock is a 4% yield.

### Why Did My Dividend Yield Go Up?

Usually because the share price fell while the dividend stayed the same, or because the company raised its dividend. Check which one changed.

### Is a Higher Dividend Yield Always Better?

No. A high yield can signal a falling price or a payout the company may not sustain. Look at the business and the dividend history too.
