# Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA: Tax Rules, Limits and Examples for 2026

Two retirement accounts can hold the same investments and still leave a saver with different amounts after tax. The main difference in the Roth IRA vs traditional IRA choice is not what you can buy inside them. It is when the tax is paid.

A traditional IRA may give a deduction now, with tax due on withdrawals later. A Roth IRA gives no deduction now, but qualified withdrawals can be tax-free. Income limits, workplace plan coverage and withdrawal rules all change the picture. This page sets out the 2026 IRS rules, works through labeled examples, and explains why the answer depends on tax rates more than on age.

## Key Takeaways

- **One shared limit:** the IRS set the 2026 IRA contribution limit at $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up for people 50 and older, across all traditional and Roth IRAs combined.
- **Roth income limits apply:** for 2026, the Roth contribution phase-out range is $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly.
- **Tax timing is the key difference:** traditional contributions may be deductible with taxable withdrawals; Roth contributions are not deductible, and qualified distributions are tax-free.
- **Required distributions differ:** traditional IRAs carry required minimum distributions; the IRS says Roth IRAs do not require them for the original owner.
- **Equal tax rates, equal results:** in a simple model with the same tax rate now and later, both accounts leave the same after-tax amount.

## What Is the Main Difference Between Roth and Traditional IRAs?

The [IRS comparison of traditional and Roth IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras) sets out the core rules. Traditional IRA contributions can be deducted if the saver qualifies, and deductible contributions and earnings are taxed when withdrawn. Roth contributions are not deductible, and qualified distributions are not taxed.

| Feature | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Deduction for contributions | Yes, if eligible | No |
| Tax on qualified withdrawals | Deductible contributions and earnings taxed | None |
| 2026 contribution limit | $7,500 combined across IRAs | Same combined limit |
| 2026 catch-up, age 50+ | $1,100 | Same combined catch-up |
| Income limit on contributing | No, but deduction may phase out | Yes, contributions phase out |
| Required minimum distributions | Yes | Not for the original owner |
| Early withdrawal before 59½ | May owe 10% additional tax | May owe tax and 10% on earnings unless qualified |

Source: IRS, Traditional and Roth IRAs; IRS 2026 limit announcement.

### Same Investments, Different Wrapper

Both accounts are tax wrappers, not investments. Either can hold stock funds, bond funds or cash, depending on the provider. The choice between them is about tax treatment, not about which investments perform better. Moving the same fund from one wrapper to the other changes only when and how its gains are taxed.

### Contribution Rules

Either account requires taxable compensation, such as wages, for the saver or a spouse filing jointly. The IRS says a traditional IRA has no age limit for contributions since 2020, and Roth contributions can be made at any age if income is below the limits.

## What Are the 2026 Contribution Limits?

The [IRS's 2026 cost-of-living announcement](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500) raised the IRA limit to $7,500 and the catch-up for those 50 and older to $1,100. The limit covers all traditional and Roth IRAs together, not each one separately.

| 2026 IRA rule | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Combined contribution limit | $7,500 |
| Additional catch-up, age 50+ | $1,100 |
| Roth phase-out, single filers | $153,000 to $168,000 |
| Roth phase-out, married filing jointly | $242,000 to $252,000 |

Source: IRS, 2026 retirement plan limits announcement.

### What the Phase-Out Means

Below the lower figure, a saver can make a full Roth contribution. Inside the range, the allowed amount shrinks. Above the upper figure, direct Roth contributions are not allowed. The income measure is modified adjusted gross income, which the IRS defines for this purpose.

### Deduction Limits for Traditional IRAs

Anyone with compensation can contribute to a traditional IRA, but the deduction may be reduced or lost if the saver or a spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan and income is above IRS thresholds. A nondeductible contribution creates basis that is not taxed again on withdrawal. Keeping careful records of nondeductible contributions helps show which part of a later withdrawal has already been taxed.

## Which Saves More Tax?

There is no universal winner in the Roth IRA vs traditional IRA comparison. A deductible traditional contribution lowers taxable income now. A Roth contribution uses after-tax money but can shield qualified withdrawals later. The relative benefit depends on the tax rate today and the rate that applies when money comes out.

### A Labeled Example With Different Rates

Assume a worker sets aside $5,000 of pre-tax income for retirement, faces a hypothetical 22% marginal rate today and a hypothetical 12% rate in retirement. State taxes are ignored, the deduction is assumed to be available, and both accounts double in value purely to show the tax effect.

| Hypothetical path | Contribution | Value after doubling | After-tax value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Traditional, 12% tax at withdrawal | $5,000 | $10,000 | $8,800 |
| Roth, 22% tax paid up front | $3,900 | $7,800 | $7,800 |

In this case the traditional account ends $1,000 ahead because the withdrawal tax rate is lower than today's rate.

### The Same Example With Equal Rates

If the rate in retirement were also 22%, the traditional account would leave $10,000 minus 22%, or $7,800, exactly matching the Roth. When tax rates are equal, the two accounts give the same result in this simple model. If the later rate were higher than today's, the Roth would come out ahead.

## A 30-Year Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA Example

Longer horizons make the tax effect larger in dollars. This hypothetical case assumes a saver contributes the full $7,500 every year for 30 years, earns a steady 6% a year, and makes each deposit at year-end. Returns, limits and tax rates are held fixed purely for illustration.

| Hypothetical case | Balance after 30 years | After-tax value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Roth, $7,500 a year after tax | About $592,936 | About $592,936 |
| Traditional, taxed at 12% on withdrawal | About $592,936 | About $521,784 |
| Traditional, taxed at 22% on withdrawal | About $592,936 | About $462,490 |

The balance formula is $7,500 × [(1.06^30 − 1) ÷ 0.06]. These are not forecasts.

### What This Example Shows

When the full limit goes into each account, the Roth holds more after-tax money because its tax was paid before deposit. The traditional saver, though, kept a tax saving each year that this table does not count. If that saving were invested in a taxable account, the gap would narrow.

### What It Leaves Out

The example ignores state taxes, fees, investment losses, changes in the limit and changes in tax law. Real returns vary every year. The table only shows how tax timing changes the after-tax balance when every other input is held constant.

## How Tax Brackets Shape the Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA Choice

The comparison turns on two marginal tax rates: the rate saved by deducting a contribution today and the rate paid on withdrawals later. If today's rate is higher, the traditional account usually wins. If the later rate is higher, the Roth usually wins.

Future rates depend on retirement income, which includes Social Security, pensions, required distributions and any part-time work. A saver with large traditional balances could face higher taxable income in retirement than expected.

### Low-Income Years

Years with unusually low income, such as early career, a gap between jobs or the first years of retirement, can favor Roth contributions. Tax paid in a low bracket may be less than tax paid later in a higher one.

### High-Income Years

Peak earning years often favor traditional contributions, because each deducted dollar saves tax at a high marginal rate. For incomes above the Roth phase-out, direct Roth contributions are not allowed at all, which settles the question for that tax year.

## Why Equal Deposits Can Mislead

Comparing a $7,500 Roth deposit with a $7,500 traditional deposit is not like with like. The Roth deposit is made with after-tax money, so it represents more pre-tax income. The traditional deposit produces a tax saving that could be spent, saved or invested elsewhere.

A fair comparison decides what is held constant: the amount deposited, the pre-tax income set aside, or the household's spending. It must also count the tax due on traditional withdrawals.

### When the Limit Is the Constraint

For a saver who contributes the full $7,500, a Roth effectively shelters more money, because the tax has already been paid. That can favor the Roth for people who max out and expect similar tax rates later. It is one reason high savers often look closely at the Roth option.

### Overstating Either Side

Comparisons that ignore what happens to the traditional tax saving can overstate the Roth's advantage. Comparisons that assume retirement tax rates will always be lower can overstate the traditional advantage. Neither assumption holds for everyone, and tax law itself can change over a working life.

## When Are Roth Withdrawals Tax-Free?

Not every Roth withdrawal is tax-free. The [IRS Roth IRA page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-iras) says contributions are not deductible and qualified distributions are tax-free. The IRS also says qualified distributions are tax-free; other withdrawals may be partly taxable, and those under age 59½ may owe an additional 10% tax unless an exception applies. Regular contributions, conversions and earnings are treated differently.

The full rules for what makes a distribution qualified, including holding-period and qualifying-event conditions, are in IRS Publication 590-B. Savers planning early withdrawals should check those rules or ask a tax professional before acting.

### Traditional IRA Withdrawals

The IRS says deductible contributions and earnings withdrawn from a traditional IRA are taxable, and withdrawals before age 59½ may bring a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies. If the account holds nondeductible contributions, part of each withdrawal may be tax-free.

### Required Minimum Distributions

Traditional IRA owners must begin taking required minimum distributions at an age set by law, and the starting age has changed in recent years. Roth IRAs do not require distributions for the original owner, according to the IRS. That gives Roth owners more control over when to draw down.

## How Do Workplace Plans Fit In?

Many workers also have a 401(k) or similar plan. Capturing an employer match is often treated as a first step before deciding how to split extra savings between account types. FinLoom's [401(k) match statistics](/investing/401k-match-statistics/) explain how match formulas work.

Some workplace plans offer a Roth option too. The same tax-rate logic applies: a Roth 401(k) taxes money now, a traditional 401(k) taxes it later. Workplace plans have much higher limits than IRAs: the IRS set the 2026 employee deferral limit for 401(k) plans at $24,500, more than three times the $7,500 IRA limit. A saver who wants to put away more than the IRA allows will usually need a workplace plan, a taxable account or both, and the same tax-timing question applies inside each one.

### Spreading Tax Risk

Holding both Roth and traditional money gives flexibility later. In retirement, a saver can choose which account to draw from in a given year, which can help manage taxable income. This is sometimes called tax diversification, and it can be useful when future tax rates are hard to predict.

### Questions to Ask

Is my tax rate likely to be higher or lower in retirement? Can I deduct a traditional contribution? Is my income near the Roth phase-out? Will I need the money before 59½? Do I want to avoid required distributions? Honest answers usually point toward one side of the Roth IRA vs traditional IRA choice.

### Splitting Contributions

Savers do not have to choose only one. The $7,500 limit can be divided between a Roth and a traditional IRA in the same year. Splitting can hedge against uncertainty about future tax rates, though the combined total cannot exceed the limit. A simple split, such as half in each, is easy to keep up and can be adjusted in later years as income and tax rates become clearer.

## Common Mistakes in the Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA Decision

The most common mistake is assuming younger savers should always choose a Roth. Age affects time horizon, but tax rates, deduction eligibility and income decide the comparison. A young worker in a high bracket may gain more from a deduction now.

A second mistake is contributing the full limit to each account. The $7,500 limit is shared across all IRAs, so excess contributions can bring IRS penalties.

### Missing the Income Limits

A raise or bonus can push income into or past the Roth phase-out range during the year. Checking income before contributing, or contributing after the year's income is known, helps avoid an excess contribution that must be corrected before the tax deadline.

### Forgetting State Taxes

The examples on this page ignore state income tax. A saver who plans to move from a high-tax state to a no-tax state in retirement faces a different calculation from one who expects to stay. State rules can tilt the Roth IRA vs traditional IRA answer either way.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I Have Both a Roth and a Traditional IRA?

Yes. You can hold both, but contributions to all your IRAs share one annual limit, which is $7,500 in 2026, plus a $1,100 catch-up at age 50 or older.

### Is a Roth Always Better for Younger Investors?

No. Age affects the time horizon, but current and future tax rates, deduction eligibility and income limits decide which account leaves more after tax.

### What Are the 2026 Roth IRA Income Limits?

The IRS set the 2026 phase-out range at $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly.

### Are Roth IRA Withdrawals Always Tax-Free?

No. Only qualified distributions are tax-free. Other withdrawals may be partly taxable, and those before age 59½ may face an additional 10% tax unless an exception applies.

### Do Roth IRAs Have Required Minimum Distributions?

The IRS says Roth IRAs do not require minimum distributions for the original owner. Traditional IRAs do require them once the owner reaches the age set by law.
