# HSA vs PPO: An Account vs a Network, and How to Compare Plans

Benefits enrollment screens often present two choices that look like rivals: an "HSA plan" and a "PPO plan." The HSA vs PPO framing is convenient, but it compares two different kinds of things. One is a savings account. The other is a way an insurance plan organizes its doctors and hospitals.

A health savings account (HSA) is a tax-advantaged account for qualified medical expenses. A preferred provider organization (PPO) is a type of health plan with a network of providers. What decides whether you can contribute to an HSA is whether your plan qualifies as a high deductible health plan (HDHP), not whether it carries the PPO label. This guide explains the difference using IRS and HealthCare.gov sources reviewed on November 21, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Different categories:** an HSA is an account, while a PPO is a health plan network design.
- **Eligibility follows the plan:** you need coverage under a qualifying HDHP, among other rules, to contribute to an HSA.
- **2026 limits:** the IRS sets HSA contribution limits of $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage.
- **2026 HDHP thresholds:** minimum deductibles are $1,700 and $3,400, with out-of-pocket maximums of $8,500 and $17,000.
- **HSAs stay with you:** IRS Publication 969 says an HSA is portable and balances carry over from year to year.
- **Compare whole plans:** premiums, deductibles, employer contributions, networks and expected care decide the real cost.

## What Is the Difference Between an HSA and a PPO?

An HSA is a financial account. HealthCare.gov describes it as a type of savings account that lets you set aside money on a pre-tax basis to pay for qualified medical expenses, such as deductibles, copayments and coinsurance. The account belongs to you, and the money in it can be saved or spent on qualified costs.

A PPO is a plan design. HealthCare.gov's glossary defines a preferred provider organization as a type of health plan that contracts with medical providers, such as hospitals and doctors, to create a network. You pay less when you use providers in the network, and you can use providers outside it for an additional cost.

### Why the Labels Get Mixed Up

Employers often name plans by their most visible feature. A plan with a high deductible and an HSA attached becomes "the HSA plan." A plan with a lower deductible and a broad network becomes "the PPO plan." Both plans may in fact use a PPO network. The names describe marketing, not the legal tests for HSA eligibility.

That is why the HSA vs PPO choice can mislead. The real comparison is between two specific insurance plans, each with its own premium, deductible, network and rules. One may allow HSA contributions and the other may not. The PPO label on either plan does not settle that question.

### How the Pieces Fit Together

Think of three layers. The network type, such as PPO or HMO, decides which providers you can see and at what cost. The deductible and out-of-pocket structure decide how much you pay before the plan pays more. The account, such as an HSA, decides how you save for and pay those costs with tax benefits.

A single plan can combine these layers in different ways. An HDHP can use a PPO network and qualify for an HSA. A low-deductible PPO typically will not qualify, because its deductible falls below the IRS minimum. Reading each plan's summary of benefits shows how its layers are set up.

## HSA vs PPO Side by Side

The table below sets out how the two terms differ. It describes general rules from IRS Publication 969 and HealthCare.gov, not the terms of any specific employer or Marketplace plan.

| Feature | HSA | PPO |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Tax-advantaged savings account | Health plan with a provider network |
| Who owns it | The account holder | The insurer runs the plan |
| Unused money | Carries over year to year | No account balance |
| Pays premiums | Generally no, with listed exceptions | Premiums fund the plan |
| Network rules | None, it is not insurance | In-network costs less, out-of-network allowed at extra cost |
| Decides HSA eligibility | No, the plan type does | The PPO label alone does not |

Read row by row, the HSA vs PPO table shows two tools doing separate jobs.

### Reading the Table

Most rows show that the two items do different jobs. An HSA holds money. A PPO organizes care. The only point where they meet is eligibility: you can contribute to an HSA only while covered by a qualifying HDHP. Whether that HDHP uses a PPO network is a separate detail.

The premium row is easy to misread. HealthCare.gov says HSA funds generally may not be used to pay premiums. Publication 969 lists exceptions, including long-term care insurance, continuation coverage such as COBRA, coverage while receiving unemployment compensation, and Medicare and other coverage if you are 65 or older, other than Medigap premiums.

### Can a PPO Be HSA-Eligible?

Yes, if it meets the HDHP rules. HealthCare.gov's guide to [HSA-eligible plans](https://www.healthcare.gov/high-deductible-health-plan/hdhp-hsa-information/) says all Bronze and Catastrophic Marketplace plans work with HSAs, and plans in other categories may also qualify depending on their deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums. Employer plans follow the same IRS tests.

The page adds that a qualifying plan generally cannot cover most non-preventive services until the deductible is met, while preventive care is still covered before the deductible. When in doubt, ask the employer or insurer for written confirmation that a specific plan is HSA-eligible rather than relying on its name.

## The 2026 HSA and HDHP Numbers

The IRS sets HSA and HDHP figures each year. For 2026, they come from [Revenue Procedure 2025-19](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-21_IRB), published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2025-21. The table below lists the 2026 values exactly as the revenue procedure states them.

| 2026 Figure | Self-Only Coverage | Family Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| HSA contribution limit | $4,400 | $8,750 |
| HDHP minimum annual deductible | $1,700 | $3,400 |
| HDHP maximum out-of-pocket expenses | $8,500 | $17,000 |

These three lines decide whether a plan in any HSA vs PPO decision can be paired with an HSA in 2026.

### What the Thresholds Mean

The deductible floor is the main test. A plan with a deductible below $1,700 for self-only coverage, or $3,400 for family coverage, does not meet the 2026 HDHP definition. The out-of-pocket cap works the other way: a plan whose out-of-pocket expenses can exceed $8,500 or $17,000 also fails the test.

Revenue Procedure 2025-19 says out-of-pocket expenses for this purpose include deductibles, copayments and other amounts, but not premiums. That matches Publication 969's description of an HDHP as having a higher deductible than typical plans and a maximum limit on the sum of the deductible and out-of-pocket expenses for covered care.

### Catch-Up Contributions

Publication 969 says an eligible individual who is 55 or older at the end of the tax year can contribute an extra $1,000. If both spouses are 55 or older and not enrolled in Medicare, each can make the additional contribution, but each must put it in their own HSA. The IRS notes that spouses cannot share a joint HSA.

Contributions stop once Medicare starts. Publication 969 says that beginning with the first month you are enrolled in Medicare, your contribution limit is zero, and this applies to periods of retroactive Medicare coverage too. People nearing 65 who delay Medicare should check how backdated coverage could affect contributions they have already made.

## Who Can Contribute to an HSA?

[IRS Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) lists four requirements. You must be covered under an HDHP on the first day of the month. You must have no other health coverage except what the rules permit. You must not be enrolled in Medicare. And you must not be claimable as a dependent on someone else's tax return.

The IRS adds some detail. You can still be eligible if your spouse has non-HDHP family coverage that does not cover you. You may also remain eligible if you receive care from the Department of Veterans Affairs for a service-connected disability. These exceptions show why eligibility depends on the full picture, not one plan document.

### The Last-Month Rule

Publication 969 explains a special timing rule. If you are eligible on the first day of the last month of your tax year, which is December 1 for most taxpayers, you can be treated as eligible for the entire year. That lets someone who joins an HDHP late in the year contribute the full annual amount.

The rule has a catch. You must remain eligible during a testing period that runs through the end of the following year. If you fail to remain eligible for reasons other than death or disability, the extra contributions become taxable income and face a 10% additional tax, calculated on Form 8889.

### What Happens With Excess Contributions

Contributing too much has a cost. Publication 969 says you generally must pay a 6% excise tax on excess contributions, and the tax applies for each year the excess stays in the account. Contributions from you, your employer and anyone else all count toward the annual limit.

This counts in an HSA vs PPO decision because employer money is part of the limit. If your employer adds $600 to your HSA, you have $600 less room under the cap. Checking payroll contributions against the 2026 limits before year end helps avoid an excise tax that is easy to miss.

## What an HSA Offers Beyond the Plan Year

Publication 969 lists the main benefits. You can deduct contributions you make even if you do not itemize. Employer contributions may be excluded from income. Contributions stay in the account until you use them. Interest and other earnings in the account are tax free. And distributions can be tax free when used for qualified medical expenses.

The IRS also calls the HSA portable. It stays with you if you change employers or leave the workforce. Publication 969 says amounts left at the end of the year generally carry over to the next year. That is a major difference from a use-it-or-lose-it arrangement such as some flexible spending accounts.

### Using HSA Money for Other Purposes

Publication 969 says you can take a distribution at any time, but only amounts used for qualified medical expenses are tax free. Distributions for other reasons are subject to income tax and may be subject to an additional 20% tax. That penalty makes an HSA a poor place for money you might need for non-medical costs.

The account's long-term role depends on cash flow. Someone who can pay current medical bills from other savings may leave HSA money untouched. Someone who relies on the HSA for this year's deductible needs that cash available. The [HSA investment options](/money-banking/hsa-investment-options/) guide looks at how account holders weigh cash and investments inside the account.

### How HSAs Compare With Other Accounts

An HSA is one of several health accounts an employer may offer. Flexible spending accounts and health reimbursement arrangements follow different rules on ownership, carryover and eligibility. The [HSA vs FSA](/money-banking/hsa-vs-fsa/) comparison sets out how those two accounts differ, including which one stays with you after a job change.

These choices often appear on the same enrollment screen. Understanding which account pairs with which plan helps avoid surprises, since other health coverage can affect HSA eligibility. Plan documents and the benefits administrator can confirm how the options interact at a given employer.

## How to Compare Two Benefit Plans

The useful comparison is not HSA vs PPO in the abstract. It is one specific plan against another, with all the costs added up. A full comparison looks at yearly premiums, the deductible, coinsurance and copays, the out-of-pocket maximum, any employer HSA contribution, the network and expected care.

Here is a hypothetical comparison, not a quote for any real plan. It assumes expected out-of-pocket spending for a typical year. Real costs depend on the care a household uses and the prices each plan has negotiated.

| Annual Input | Plan A (HSA-Eligible HDHP) | Plan B (Lower-Deductible PPO) |
|---|---|---|
| Employee premiums | $2,400 | $4,200 |
| Expected out-of-pocket spending | $2,800 | $1,200 |
| Employer HSA contribution | $600 | $0 |
| Illustrative net cost | $4,600 | $5,400 |

The last row adds the first three together, so each step of this HSA vs PPO example can be checked by hand.

### Working Through the Example

In this example, Plan A costs $2,400 plus $2,800, minus the $600 employer contribution, for $4,600. Plan B costs $4,200 plus $1,200, for $5,400. On these assumptions, Plan A costs $800 less for the year. The employer contribution is treated as money that offsets costs, even though it sits in the employee's own account.

The table leaves out several things on purpose. It does not include the tax value of HSA contributions, which varies by income. It does not model the timing of medical bills versus paychecks. And it uses a single expected spending figure, when real spending is uncertain. These gaps are why the result is an illustration, not a recommendation.

### Testing a High-Cost Year

A second scenario helps. Instead of a typical year, assume a year with major care that reaches each plan's out-of-pocket maximum. The plan with the lower premium but higher maximum can then cost more. HealthCare.gov's out-of-pocket maximum guidance notes the limit does not include premiums, out-of-network care or services the plan does not cover.

Running both a typical year and a bad year gives a range rather than a single answer. A household with ongoing treatment, regular prescriptions or a preferred specialist may weigh network access and the worst case more heavily than the average. Plan summaries list each figure needed for this second scenario.

## Network Access and Out-of-Network Costs

The PPO part of a plan decides where you can get care and at what price. HealthCare.gov's PPO definition says you pay less with network providers and can go outside the network for an additional cost. An HDHP that uses a PPO network keeps that flexibility. An HDHP built on a narrower network may not.

Out-of-network care can be costly because it may not count toward the out-of-pocket limit. Checking whether your current doctors, hospitals and pharmacies are in a plan's network is often more important than a small premium difference. Provider directories change, so confirming directly with a provider before enrolling is a sensible step.

### Prescriptions and Preventive Care

Prescription costs can shift the comparison. On an HDHP, most prescriptions are paid at full negotiated cost until the deductible is met, unless the plan covers them as preventive care. On a lower-deductible plan, copays may apply sooner. Checking the plan's drug list for any regular medication gives a clearer picture.

Preventive care follows its own rule. Publication 969 says an HDHP may provide preventive care benefits without a deductible. It lists examples such as annual physicals, routine prenatal and well-child care, immunizations and screening services. Knowing what counts as preventive under a specific plan helps estimate first-dollar costs.

### Matching the Plan to the Household

Every household is different. A single person who rarely sees a doctor faces a different trade-off than a family expecting a birth or managing a chronic condition. Neither plan type is better for everyone. FinLoom does not recommend a specific plan; the goal is to show which numbers drive the result.

Health coverage also connects to wider money decisions. Choosing whether to keep cash available or invest for later is a core trade-off covered in the [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) guide. The same question applies inside an HSA, where some money may be needed this year and some may be left to grow.

## The Bottom Line

The HSA vs PPO question compares an account with a network type. What decides HSA eligibility is whether the plan is a qualifying HDHP, and a PPO can be one. For 2026, the IRS sets HSA limits of $4,400 and $8,750 and HDHP deductible floors of $1,700 and $3,400. Compare whole plans, including premiums, employer money, networks and a high-cost year, before choosing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an HSA a Type of Health Insurance?

No. An HSA is a tax-advantaged savings account for qualified medical expenses. You need coverage under a qualifying high deductible health plan to contribute to one.

### Can a PPO Plan Be HSA-Eligible?

Yes. A PPO can qualify if it meets the IRS high deductible health plan rules. For 2026, that means a deductible of at least $1,700 self-only or $3,400 family.

### What Are the 2026 HSA Contribution Limits?

Revenue Procedure 2025-19 sets the 2026 limits at $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. People 55 or older can add $1,000.

### Can I Use HSA Money to Pay Premiums?

Generally no. Publication 969 lists exceptions, including long-term care insurance, COBRA, coverage during unemployment, and Medicare premiums at 65 or older, excluding Medigap.

### Does My HSA Stay With Me If I Change Jobs?

Yes. IRS Publication 969 says an HSA is portable and stays with you if you change employers or leave the workforce. Unused balances carry over each year.
