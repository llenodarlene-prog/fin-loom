# HSA vs FSA in 2026: Ownership, Eligibility, Rollover, and Tax Rules

The HSA vs FSA question comes up every open enrollment, and the two accounts are easy to confuse. Both can lower the after-tax cost of medical bills, but they are not interchangeable. The biggest difference is what happens to the money at the end of the year or when you change jobs.

A health savings account (HSA) belongs to you and carries forward. A health flexible spending arrangement (FSA) runs through your employer's plan and has stricter use-it-or-lose-it rules. Choosing between them is not only about which has a higher limit. Eligibility, timing, expected medical bills and the type of FSA your employer offers all shape the answer.

## Key Takeaways

- An HSA is owned by you and stays with you after a job change, while an FSA runs through your employer's plan.
- For 2026, the HSA limit is $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage, and the FSA salary-reduction limit is $3,400.
- An FSA can let up to $680 of unused 2026 money carry into the next plan year, or offer a grace period of up to 2½ months, but not both.
- A health FSA must make your full yearly election available at any time during the coverage period.
- A general-purpose FSA usually blocks HSA contributions, while limited-purpose and post-deductible FSAs can work alongside one.

## HSA vs FSA at a Glance

The table puts the main HSA vs FSA differences side by side. Figures are the 2026 limits from IRS guidance, and individual plans can be stricter.

| Feature | Health savings account (HSA) | General-purpose health FSA |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it up | An eligible individual, often with employer help | An employer, through a cafeteria plan |
| Who owns it | You | Your employer's plan |
| 2026 limit | $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family, plus $1,000 at 55+ | $3,400 employee salary reduction |
| Unused money at year-end | Stays in the account | Forfeited unless the plan offers a carryover or grace period |
| 2026 carryover cap | Not applicable | Up to $680 if the plan allows it |
| Investing | Offered by many HSA providers | Not an investment account |
| Changing jobs | The account stays with you | Depends on plan rules |
| When money is available | As it is deposited | Full yearly election available during the coverage period |

The limits come from [IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-19](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-21_IRB) for HSAs and the [2026 inflation adjustments in IRS Bulletin 2025-45](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-irbs/irb25-45.pdf) for FSAs. Employer plans can set lower carryover amounts or offer neither option.

## What Makes an HSA Different?

An HSA can be opened by anyone who meets the eligibility rules. Contributions get federal tax advantages, qualified medical withdrawals are generally tax-free, and the balance can stay in cash or be invested for years. The account is portable, so a job change does not wipe out the balance.

To contribute, you generally need coverage under a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP), no disqualifying other health coverage, no Medicare enrollment, and no one able to claim you as a dependent. The 2026 HDHP thresholds are below.

| 2026 HDHP rule | Self-only | Family |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum annual deductible | $1,700 | $3,400 |
| Maximum out-of-pocket | $8,500 | $17,000 |
| HSA contribution limit | $4,400 | $8,750 |

IRS Publication 969 says an eligible person aged 55 or older at the end of the tax year can add $1,000. Employer deposits count toward the same limit, and the limit is reduced for months when you are not eligible.

## How a Health FSA Works

A health FSA is usually offered through an employer's cafeteria plan. You choose a salary-reduction amount during benefits enrollment, and the money reimburses qualified medical costs during the plan year. Because it is not a personal investment account, it lacks the HSA's long-term investing and portability.

One feature is especially useful for someone with a known upcoming expense. Publication 969 says you must be able to receive the maximum amount you elected at any time during the coverage period, regardless of how much you have contributed so far. An HSA, by contrast, can only pay out money already in the account.

The 2026 employee salary-reduction limit for health FSAs is $3,400. That figure covers your own election. Employer contributions follow separate plan rules.

## Which Balance Rolls Over?

An HSA has no use-it-or-lose-it deadline. Unspent money stays in the account, and you can keep using it for qualified expenses after leaving a job. An FSA works differently, and the details depend on which of two options your employer picks, if either.

### The FSA Carryover Option

For plans that allow it, up to $680 of unused 2026 money can be used for qualified expenses in the following plan year. An employer can set a lower cap. Any amount above the carryover cap is forfeited. A plan with a carryover cannot also offer a grace period for the same health FSA.

### The FSA Grace Period Option

Instead of a carryover, a plan can offer a grace period of up to 2½ months after the plan year ends. Qualified expenses you incur during that window can be paid from money left over from the previous year. Some plans offer neither option, so your benefits documents are the final word.

For someone unsure about future medical spending, these rules shape how much to elect. A large FSA election brings immediate tax savings, but unused money can be lost if expected costs never arrive and the plan's rules do not save it.

## Can You Have an HSA and FSA at the Same Time?

Sometimes, but the type of FSA decides it. [IRS Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) says an employee covered by an HDHP and a health FSA or HRA that pays qualified medical expenses generally cannot contribute to an HSA. This often catches people out when a spouse enrolls in a general-purpose FSA that covers the whole family.

### Limited-Purpose FSAs

A limited-purpose FSA can sit alongside an HSA. Publication 969 says these arrangements can pay for the items listed under other health coverage, such as dental and vision care, but not long-term care, along with preventive care. Because they do not pay general medical bills before the deductible, they do not block HSA contributions.

### Post-Deductible FSAs

A post-deductible FSA does not pay any medical expenses incurred before the HDHP's minimum annual deductible is met. That design also lets an employee keep contributing to an HSA. Publication 969 adds that coverage during a general-purpose FSA's grace period is allowed only if the FSA balance at the end of the prior plan year was zero.

Account names alone do not settle eligibility. Read your employer's plan documents and confirm the FSA type before you contribute to both. Getting it wrong can create an excess HSA contribution that needs fixing. If you discover the problem mid-year, ask your HSA provider and your employer how to withdraw or correct the excess before your tax filing deadline, because Publication 969 says excess contributions generally face a 6% excise tax for each tax year they remain in the account. Withdrawing the excess in time, under the rules the publication sets out, can avoid that tax on the amount withdrawn. A short check before enrollment avoids that paperwork altogether.

## How Do the Tax Benefits Compare?

Both accounts can reduce taxable income when funded correctly, and both can pay qualified medical costs without federal tax. The HSA goes further: investment earnings grow tax-advantaged, qualified withdrawals stay tax-free over many years, and the money stays with you after a job change.

There are limits on both sides. State tax treatment of HSAs can differ from federal treatment. An expense must meet the eligibility rules to be reimbursed tax-free, and the same bill cannot be reimbursed twice or also claimed as a deduction.

Publication 969 also explains the HSA's penalty for misuse. Non-medical HSA withdrawals are added to income and may face an extra 20% tax, but that additional tax does not apply after age 65, disability or death.

## Example: An Expected $2,000 Medical Expense

Suppose an employee expects $2,000 of qualifying treatment early in the plan year. If the employer offers an FSA and the employee elects $2,000, the plan must make that full amount available during the coverage period, so the bill can be reimbursed before most payroll deductions have been taken. That timing helps someone facing an early-year expense.

An HSA can be more flexible over time because unused money carries forward, but it can only pay out what is already in the account. If the employee has just started contributing, the HSA balance may not yet cover the bill. The better tool depends on eligibility, timing and whether the worker wants to build savings for future years.

## Example: Uncertain Medical Costs

Now imagine someone with low, unpredictable health spending who wants a cushion for surprises. An HSA balance carries into future years and, depending on the provider, can be invested. That helps if the person is eligible and can live with market swings without selling at a bad time.

A general-purpose FSA can still be valuable for predictable costs, especially when an employer contributes. But this person should elect only an amount they can reasonably spend under the plan's carryover or grace-period rules. Treating the full $3,400 limit as a target can create unnecessary forfeiture risk.

## HSA vs FSA at Different Life Stages

The HSA vs FSA answer often shifts as your health needs and income change. These three situations show how the same rules can point in different directions.

### Early Career With Few Medical Costs

A young employee with low, predictable health spending who is enrolled in an HSA-eligible HDHP may get the most from an HSA. Small contributions carry forward each year, and over time the balance can build a cushion for later costs. A general-purpose FSA risks forfeiting money this person never needed to spend.

### A Family With Regular Medical Bills

A household with steady prescriptions, therapy visits or orthodontic work may value an FSA's uniform coverage, which makes the full election available early in the plan year. If the family's plan is also HSA-eligible, a limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision costs can work next to an HSA for other expenses.

### Approaching Retirement

Someone nearing 65 should plan around two HSA rules. Enrolling in Medicare ends eligibility to make new HSA contributions, but Publication 969 says the extra 20% tax on non-medical withdrawals no longer applies after age 65. An existing HSA balance can still pay qualified medical costs in retirement.

## What to Check During Open Enrollment

The HSA vs FSA decision is easiest when you work through it in order. These steps keep the focus on facts you can confirm:

1. **Confirm your health plan.** Check whether it is an HSA-eligible HDHP for 2026.
2. **Identify the FSA type.** Ask whether it is general-purpose, limited-purpose or post-deductible.
3. **Read the year-end rule.** Find out whether the plan offers a carryover, a grace period or neither.
4. **Check employer money.** Note any employer HSA deposit or FSA contribution.
5. **Check your spouse's benefits.** A spouse's general-purpose FSA covering you can affect your HSA eligibility.

Then compare current costs with future flexibility, and write down the amount you expect to spend before you pick a number for the FSA. A plan that helps with next month's treatment may differ from an account built for long-term health savings.

## A Practical Way to Choose

The HSA vs FSA decision begins with eligibility, not a ranking. An HSA is usually more flexible for eligible people who want to keep unused health money, while an FSA can suit employees with known near-term costs and employer-sponsored benefits. Where a limited-purpose FSA is available, the two can work together rather than compete.

The same thinking applies to any short-term versus long-term money decision. FinLoom's guide to [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) explains how time horizon and liquidity decide where money should sit, which is the same trade-off an HSA owner faces between cash and investments.

Before electing either account, let the plan documents do the work. Confirm which expenses qualify, how much you can contribute, when money becomes available, and what happens if your job or health plan changes during the year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I Use an HSA and a General-Purpose FSA Together?

Generally no. A general-purpose FSA usually blocks HSA contributions while it covers you. Limited-purpose or post-deductible FSAs can work alongside an HSA, depending on the plan's design.

### What Is the 2026 HSA Contribution Limit?

The IRS set $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. Eligible people aged 55 or older at year-end can add $1,000, subject to the other HSA rules.

### What Is the 2026 Health FSA Limit?

The employee salary-reduction limit for health FSAs is $3,400 for 2026. Employer contributions and the benefits available depend on the terms of your employer's specific cafeteria plan.

### Does Unused FSA Money Always Disappear?

No. A plan may allow up to $680 to carry into the next year, or offer a grace period of up to 2½ months, but some plans offer neither option.

### Does an HSA Expire When I Change Jobs?

No. HSA money stays with you. New contributions still require eligibility, and fees or investment options may change if your former employer was paying some of the account costs.

### Is HSA vs FSA a Choice I Make Every Year?

Usually yes. FSA elections are made each plan year during enrollment, and HSA eligibility depends on your health plan each month, so review both options every open enrollment period.
