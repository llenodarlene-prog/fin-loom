# What Is a Credit Score? How Scores Work and What Affects Them

What is a credit score? It is a numerical prediction of how likely a consumer is to repay borrowed money as agreed, based on information in that person's credit reports.

Lenders can use credit scores when deciding whether to approve a mortgage, credit card, auto loan, or other credit product. Scores can also affect the interest rate, credit limit, and other terms a borrower receives.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/understand-your-credit-score/) stresses an important point: you do **not** have only one credit score. Scores can differ because lenders use different scoring models and versions, because the credit-report data behind them can differ, and because data can be pulled at different times.

## Key Takeaways

- A credit score predicts credit behavior using information in a consumer's credit reports.
- Many commonly used credit scores range from 300 to 850.
- Higher scores can make it easier to qualify for credit and may lead to better borrowing terms.
- FICO Scores use five categories: payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit, and credit mix.
- FICO says payment history carries the largest typical weight, at 35%.
- Consumers can have several credit scores at the same time.
- Income, age, and where a person lives are not used in FICO Score calculations.
- A credit score and a credit report are related but are not the same thing.

## What Does a Credit Score Measure?

The simplest answer to what is a credit score is that it estimates the likelihood of future credit behavior. The CFPB describes it as a prediction, based on the information in your credit reports, of how likely you are to pay back money you borrow.

The score does not represent wealth, income, or overall financial success. Someone with a high income can have a weak credit history, while someone with a more modest income can hold a strong score through steady credit management. That is why a credit score is best read as one specific signal rather than a verdict on a person's finances.

## What Is the Typical Credit Score Range?

The CFPB says many scores range from **300 to 850**, although different companies use different ranges. FICO's widely used base scores use that 300 to 850 range, and FICO groups them into the bands below.

| FICO Score Range | FICO Rating |
|---|---|
| 300 to 579 | Poor |
| 580 to 669 | Fair |
| 670 to 739 | Good |
| 740 to 799 | Very Good |
| 800 to 850 | Exceptional |

The bands give general context, but lenders do not all use the same approval thresholds.

### A Good Score Is Not a Guarantee

A "good" score does not guarantee approval, and a lower score does not guarantee denial. Lenders can also weigh income, existing debt, collateral, loan type, down payment, and other underwriting information that sits outside the credit score. The score opens the conversation rather than ending it.

### Other Models Use Other Bands

The bands above describe FICO's base scores. Other scoring models, and some industry-specific FICO versions, can use different cutoffs or ranges. When a service shows a score and a rating, it helps to check which model produced it before comparing it with a lender's requirements.

## How FICO Scores Are Calculated

FICO says its scores are calculated from the information in a consumer's credit report, grouped into five broad categories. The typical weighting appears in the table below.

| FICO Factor | Typical Weight |
|---|---:|
| Payment History | 35% |
| Amounts Owed | 30% |
| Length of Credit History | 15% |
| New Credit | 10% |
| Credit Mix | 10% |

Source: [myFICO, "What's in my FICO Scores?"](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score), checked October 20, 2026. FICO notes that the importance of each category can vary depending on the information in a person's credit report.

### Payment History Has the Largest Typical Weight

Payment history accounts for about **35%** of a typical FICO Score. It reflects whether credit obligations have been paid on time. FICO says a track record of payment tends to be the strongest predictor of whether someone will pay debts as agreed, and it notes that a few late payments are not automatically a score-killer when the overall history is good.

### Amounts Owed Account for About 30%

FICO looks at how much debt a person carries in total and how much of their available credit they are using. Using a high share of available credit can signal that a person is overextended. As an illustration, a $3,000 balance on cards with a combined $10,000 limit means 30% of available revolving credit is in use.

### Length of Credit History Accounts for About 15%

FICO considers factors such as the age of the oldest account, the age of the newest account, and the average age of all accounts. In general, a longer history gives the scoring model more evidence about how a consumer has handled credit over time, which is why newer borrowers often start with thinner files.

### New Credit Accounts for About 10%

Recent applications and newly opened accounts can affect a FICO Score. FICO says research shows that opening several new credit accounts in a short period represents greater risk. The size of the effect depends on the rest of the consumer's profile.

### Credit Mix Accounts for About 10%

Credit mix reflects experience with different kinds of credit, such as credit cards, retail accounts, installment loans, and mortgages. FICO says it is not necessary to have one of each. Opening unnecessary debt simply to broaden the mix is not a sound reason to borrow, since the category carries a modest weight.

### Why the Weights Are Typical, Not Fixed

FICO describes these percentages as typical importance, not a formula applied identically to everyone. Someone with a short history, for example, may see different categories carry more influence. The weights are best used to understand priorities, with payment history and amounts owed together making up about two-thirds.

## What Information Does Not Go Into a FICO Score?

FICO states that personal details such as income, age, and where a person lives do not factor into its scores. Its education pages also list race, color, religion, national origin, sex, and marital status among excluded information, along with salary, occupation, employer, and employment history.

That does not mean lenders ignore income or employment. A lender can weigh information separate from the credit score when deciding whether a borrower can afford a loan. This is another reason a credit score should not be treated as a complete measure of financial health.

## Why Do I Have More Than One Credit Score?

The CFPB says consumers can have several credit scores because lenders use different scoring models, because the information in each credit report can differ, and because information changes between the dates scores are calculated.

A mortgage lender, an auto lender, and a credit-card issuer may therefore see different scores for the same consumer. Even two scores carrying the FICO name can differ when they use different model versions or different credit-bureau data.

## FICO and VantageScore Are Different Scoring Systems

FICO is not the only credit-scoring system. VantageScore is another major model, developed by the three nationwide credit reporting companies.

Both systems use information from credit reports, but they can weigh that information differently and update their methods over time. The practical takeaway is that a score shown by one service may not be the exact score a particular lender uses.

## Credit Score and Credit Report Are Not the Same Thing

A credit report holds the underlying history that scoring models read. It can include open and closed accounts, balances, payment history, credit limits, collection accounts, and credit inquiries. A credit score is calculated from that information using a scoring model.

The report is the data record, and the score is a model's numerical reading of it. Fixing an error in the report can change the score, while the score itself cannot be disputed separately from the data behind it.

## How Lenders Use Credit Scores

The CFPB says companies use credit scores when making decisions about mortgages, credit cards, auto loans, and other credit products. Scores can also influence the interest rate offered, the credit limit, and other loan terms.

A higher score can make it easier to qualify and may help a borrower receive better terms, but underwriting rules vary by lender and product. The CFPB also notes that because lenders use different scores, a borrower might qualify for lower rates with one lender and not another, so it can pay to shop around.

## Why Higher Credit Scores Can Lower Borrowing Costs

Credit pricing is partly about risk. If a lender believes a borrower is more likely to repay as agreed, it may be willing to offer a lower rate than it would offer a borrower it views as riskier.

The relationship is not fixed. Interest rates also depend on market rates, loan type, loan term, down payment, and lender pricing, so a stronger score improves a borrower's position without guaranteeing the lowest available rate.

## What Can Hurt a Credit Score?

Negative effects can come from late or missed payments, high unpaid debt, using a large share of available revolving credit, many recent credit applications, collection accounts, foreclosure, bankruptcy, and other adverse information in a credit report.

The size and duration of each effect vary by scoring model and by the rest of the consumer's profile. Fraud can also damage a credit file; [identity theft statistics](/money-banking/identity-theft-statistics/) show how often compromised personal data turns into financial harm.

## What Can Help a Credit Score Over Time?

The most reliable approach is to focus on the underlying behavior rather than chase a particular number.

### Habits Scoring Models Reward

That generally means paying bills on time, keeping revolving balances manageable, avoiding unnecessary credit applications, keeping established accounts open when it makes sense, and checking credit reports for errors. These map directly onto the two largest FICO categories, payment history and amounts owed.

### How Credit Fits a Wider Plan

A solid credit history often supports cheaper borrowing, but it works alongside savings rather than replacing them. Keeping an emergency fund in an accessible account can prevent a missed payment when an unexpected bill arrives, a role [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) explains for short-term cash.

## How to Check Your Credit Reports

Consumers can request their credit reports through [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), the federally authorized source for free credit reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.

Checking a credit report does not show every score a lender might use, but it reveals the information that scoring models evaluate. Errors can be disputed with the credit reporting company and, where appropriate, with the company that supplied the information.

## A Credit Score Is a Risk Signal, Not a Financial Report Card

So what is a credit score in the end? It has one specific job: to predict aspects of credit behavior from credit-report data. It does not measure income, savings, investments, net worth, retirement readiness, or overall financial stability.

That makes it valuable for lending decisions but incomplete as a measure of a person's finances. Understanding what is a credit score, and what it leaves out, makes it easier to treat the number as one risk signal within a wider financial picture.

## How to Read the Score a Lender Shows You

Once you know what is a credit score, the next question is which score a lender is looking at. The CFPB's guidance on understanding credit scores gives the main reasons scores differ.

### Which Model Produced the Score

The CFPB notes that lenders use different scoring models and that some models are built for specific loan types. A score from a free app may come from a different model than the one a mortgage lender uses, so the two numbers can differ without either being wrong.

### Which Credit Report It Used

Each nationwide credit reporting company can hold slightly different information, and scores can be calculated on different dates. That is why the CFPB says you have many credit scores rather than one. Checking all three reports is the most direct way to see the data each score draws on.

## Questions to Ask Before Applying for Credit

Knowing what is a credit score helps most just before an application, when the score is about to be used.

### Shopping Around for Terms

The CFPB notes that because lenders use different scores, a borrower might qualify for lower rates with one lender and not another, so it can pay to shop around. Comparing offers from more than one lender can show how much the same credit history is worth in rate terms.

### Checking Reports First

Reviewing credit reports before applying gives time to dispute any errors with the reporting company. Because scores are calculated from report data, correcting a mistake can change the score a lender sees, which is easier to do before an application than after a decision has been made.

## Common Credit Score Misunderstandings

Several beliefs about what is a credit score do not match how scoring works, according to the CFPB and FICO.

### A Higher Income Raises the Score

FICO states that salary, occupation, employer, and employment history do not factor into its scores. Income can still affect whether a lender approves a loan, because lenders assess affordability separately. A raise improves a borrower's capacity to repay without changing the score itself.

### Every Lender Sees the Same Number

The CFPB says lenders use different scoring models and that your scores can differ depending on the model, the credit report used, and the date. A score from one source is a useful guide, but it may not be the exact number a particular lender sees on an application. Asking the lender which model it uses is a simple way to close that gap, and it helps set realistic expectations before applying. It also explains why a score seen at home and a lender's figure can differ.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is a Credit Score in Simple Terms?

A credit score is a number that predicts how likely you are to repay borrowed money as agreed, based mainly on the information in your credit reports.

### What Is a Good Credit Score?

FICO classifies 670 to 739 as Good and 740 to 799 as Very Good. Individual lenders can use different models and thresholds when deciding whether to approve credit.

### What Is the Highest Credit Score?

Most widely used base FICO Scores range from 300 to 850, making 850 the highest score in that range, although some other scoring models use different ranges.

### Does Checking My Own Credit Score Lower It?

No. Checking your own credit information is treated as a soft inquiry rather than an application for credit, so reviewing your own score does not lower your FICO Scores.

### Why Are My Credit Scores Different?

Scores differ because lenders use different models and versions, the data at each credit bureau can vary, and account information can change between the dates scores are calculated.
