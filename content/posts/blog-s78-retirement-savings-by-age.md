# Retirement Savings by Age: What U.S. Account Balances Reveal in 2025

Retirement savings by age tables can make one household feel ahead and another feel badly behind. Often the issue is what the numbers measure. An average balance among families who have a retirement account is not the same as retirement savings across all families, including those with no account at all.

The Federal Reserve released its 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) on October 9, 2026. It is the newest official source for these figures and replaces the 2022 tables that still circulate widely. This page sets out what the survey shows by age, how its measures are built, and how to read a benchmark without treating it as a target. All SCF dollar figures are in 2025 dollars.

## Key Takeaways

- **Averages rose for most ages:** among families with an IRA or defined contribution plan, mean balances rose from 2022 to 2025 for every age group shown except under 35.
- **The 55 to 64 group averaged $670,200:** up from $588,500 in 2022, among families holding these accounts.
- **Medians are far lower:** the conditional median retirement account value for all families holding one was $106,000 in 2025, against a mean of $451,100.
- **About 55% of families hold accounts:** retirement accounts were held by 54.9% of families in 2025, up 0.6 points from 2022.
- **Participation is broader:** 65.1% of families with a reference person aged 64 or younger took part in some retirement plan, including pensions.

## What Are the Latest Retirement Savings Statistics by Age?

The Federal Reserve's [Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2022 to 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf26.pdf) includes a table of mean retirement savings by age. It covers only families that hold an IRA or a defined contribution plan, such as a 401(k) or 403(b). Families with no such account, or with only a traditional pension, are not part of these averages, so the table describes savers rather than every household of that age.

| Age of reference person | 2016 | 2019 | 2022 | 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Under 35 | $43,800 | $38,300 | $53,800 | $48,400 |
| 35 to 44 | $135,200 | $167,500 | $154,800 | $182,400 |
| 45 to 54 | $291,300 | $323,100 | $342,700 | $415,800 |
| 55 to 64 | $504,800 | $518,200 | $588,500 | $670,200 |

Source: Federal Reserve, SCF 2025, Box 2, Table A. Mean combined IRA and defined contribution balance among families holding these accounts, in 2025 dollars.

### What Changed Since 2022

Mean balances rose for the 35 to 44, 45 to 54 and 55 to 64 groups. The under-35 group fell from $53,800 to $48,400. The Fed notes that median values, which it did not publish by age in this table, increased for all age groups.

### The Longer Trend

From 2016 to 2025, the 55 to 64 average rose from $504,800 to $670,200, and the 45 to 54 average from $291,300 to $415,800. The Fed describes these gains as part of a longer-run trend of growing retirement account balances over the past decade.

## Why Is the Median So Different From the Average?

A mean adds every balance and divides by the number of families. A median is the middle value when balances are lined up from smallest to largest. A few very large accounts can pull the mean far above the median.

The SCF's main asset table shows this gap clearly for all families holding retirement accounts, across every age.

| All families with retirement accounts | 2022 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Share of families holding | 54.3% | 54.9% | +0.6 points |
| Conditional median value | $95,200 | $106,000 | +11% |
| Conditional mean value | $365,700 | $451,100 | +23% |

Source: Federal Reserve, SCF 2025, Table 3, in 2025 dollars.

### Why the Mean Grew Faster

The mean rose 23% while the median rose 11%. That suggests larger accounts grew faster than typical ones, possibly because of rising stock values that benefit bigger balances most. The survey describes the distribution; it does not prove why individual accounts changed.

### Which Number to Use

For a sense of what a typical account holder has, the median is the better guide. For the total stock of retirement wealth, the mean is more useful. A good retirement savings by age table always names which one it shows.

## Who Is Counted?

A balance among account holders excludes the 45% of families with no retirement account. A figure across all families must include those zeros. Both are honest numbers, but they answer different questions.

The age-group means above count only families with an IRA or defined contribution plan. They are not the average retirement savings of everyone in that age group.

### Family, Not Individual

The SCF measures families, grouped by the age of a reference person. A couple's accounts are combined. That makes the figures household-level, not the balance of one worker, and it is a common source of confusion when people compare themselves to a table.

### Provider Data Is Different Again

Retirement plan providers publish average 401(k) balances for their own participants. Those cover one company's clients and one type of account. They should not be placed beside SCF family figures as if they shared a denominator, because a provider's clients are not a random sample of U.S. families.

## How Many Families Take Part in Retirement Plans?

The SCF counts a family as participating if it has an IRA, a defined contribution plan through an employer, or a defined benefit pension from a current or former employer. Its participation figures cover families with a reference person aged 64 or younger.

Overall participation was 65.1% in 2025, up slightly from 64.4% in 2022. Around 70% of families aged 45 to 64 and 66% of those aged 35 to 44 participated.

### Younger Families Are Catching Up

Among the youngest families, participation in defined contribution plans and IRAs rose from 42% in 2016 to near 50% in 2025, the sharpest gain of any age group. The Fed also notes that lower-income families made up a growing share of participation among those under 35.

### Pensions Still Count

Defined benefit pensions are held by around one-fourth of families, according to the survey. A pension can be a major retirement resource without appearing as an account balance. A household with a pension and modest 401(k) savings may be better placed than its balance alone suggests.

## What Do Plan Provider Surveys Add?

Employer plan data show how workers behave inside their plans. [Vanguard's How America Saves 2026: Small Business Edition](https://workplace.vanguard.com/insights-and-research/report/how-america-saves-small-business-edition-2026.html) reported that 73% of eligible employees participated in the plans of more than 21,000 small businesses it studied, and that combined employee and employer saving averaged 11% of pay in 2025.

Those figures describe Vanguard's small-business plan sample, not all U.S. families. They help explain saving rates within plans but use a different base from the SCF.

| Data series | Unit counted | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Federal Reserve SCF | U.S. families | How retirement resources differ across households |
| Plan provider research | Eligible workers or participants | How employees save inside participating plans |
| IRS contribution limits | Tax rules | How much a person may contribute in a year |

Two parts of that saving rate are worth separating: the employer's share and the legal ceiling on what a worker can put in.

### Matching Contributions

Employer contributions are part of the 11% combined saving rate. FinLoom's [401(k) match statistics](/investing/401k-match-statistics/) explain how match formulas work and why a headline match percentage can be misleading. A worker who misses part of an available match leaves part of that combined rate on the table.

### Limits Are Not Targets

The [IRS's 2026 limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits) set the standard 401(k) employee deferral at $24,500, with an $8,000 catch-up for most participants aged 50 or older. A legal maximum is not a realistic savings goal for every household, and few workers contribute the full amount.

## Why Do Balances Rise With Age?

Workers who have been saving longer usually have larger balances. Contributions, employer matches and investment growth all add up over time, while job changes, caregiving, debt and early withdrawals can interrupt the process.

Age alone does not measure readiness. Expected spending, other income, debt, the planned retirement age, taxes and inflation all shape whether a balance is enough.

### Why the Under-35 Average Fell

The under-35 mean fell between 2022 and 2025. That does not prove young savers lost money. The SCF samples different families in each wave, and more young families joining plans with small starting balances can pull the average down even as participation rises.

### Compounding Over Time

A simple illustration shows why time helps. At a hypothetical steady 5% annual return, $10,000 grows to about $26,533 in 20 years and about $70,400 in 40 years, before fees and taxes. Real returns vary year to year, so this is an example, not a forecast. The same arithmetic explains why the oldest age groups show the largest averages: their money has usually been invested for decades longer. It also shows why a late start can be offset partly by saving more each year, though no contribution level guarantees a particular result.

## How Retirement Savings Fit Wider SCF Trends

The 2025 survey shows several shifts around retirement saving. Stock market participation, which counts direct and indirect holdings including through retirement accounts, fell slightly from 58% of families in 2022 to 56% in 2025. The Fed said the decline came from families outside the top 10% of the income distribution.

Among families that did hold stock, median holdings grew 36%, from $56,900 to $77,400. That gain helps explain why retirement account values rose: many of those accounts hold stock funds.

| SCF 2025 measure | 2022 | 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Families holding any stock | 58% | 56% |
| Median stock holdings among holders | $56,900 | $77,400 |
| Families holding retirement accounts | 54.3% | 54.9% |
| Retirement plan participation, ages 64 and younger | 64.4% | 65.1% |

Source: Federal Reserve, SCF 2025, in 2025 dollars.

### A Widening Gap

Rising values for those who hold stock, alongside slightly lower participation, means gains were not shared evenly. Retirement savings by age figures that report only account holders can therefore look stronger than the position of families without accounts, who are left out of those averages entirely.

### Why Participation Is the First Step

A family with no account cannot benefit from market gains, employer matches or tax breaks on retirement saving. For many households, joining a plan has a larger effect than fine-tuning investments. The rise in participation among younger families is one of the survey's more encouraging findings.

## Retirement Savings by Age Compared With Net Worth

Retirement accounts are one part of family wealth. FinLoom's [net worth by age](/money-banking/net-worth-by-age-statistics/) page covers home equity, other assets and debts using the same 2025 survey.

Many families hold more wealth in home equity than in retirement accounts, and some have business assets or pensions that do not appear in account tables. Looking only at retirement savings by age can understate or overstate a household's overall position.

### Liquidity Differences

Retirement accounts often carry tax rules and penalties for early withdrawal. Home equity can only be reached by selling or borrowing. A household can have substantial wealth and still have little cash available for an emergency, which is why a separate cash reserve is common advice.

### Using Both Measures

Comparing both measures gives a fuller picture. A family behind on retirement accounts but strong in home equity faces a different choice than one with neither. The right plan depends on the whole balance sheet, including debts and expected income.

## How Should You Compare Your Balance With a Benchmark?

First identify what the benchmark includes: one employer plan, all IRA and defined contribution accounts, or total household retirement resources. Next check whether it is a mean or a median, and whether families with no account are included. Finally confirm the survey year and dollar basis.

FinLoom's [investment growth calculator](/investing/investment-growth-calculator/) lets you test how contributions and assumed returns could change a balance over time. Any return used is an assumption, not a promise.

### A Planning Starting Point

A retirement savings by age figure is a starting point for a conversation, not a grade. The pillar guide on [crypto vs stocks](/investing/crypto-vs-stocks/) covers how asset choice and risk shape long-term returns, which can count for as much as the size of a balance.

### If a Household Is Behind

Options can include raising contributions gradually, collecting any available employer match, paying down high-interest debt, or working longer where possible. The right mix depends on cash flow and obligations, and a licensed professional can help build a specific plan suited to the household.

## Common Mistakes When Reading Retirement Savings by Age Tables

The most frequent mistake is treating a mean among account holders as the typical balance for everyone of that age. In the SCF, the overall conditional mean of $451,100 is more than four times the $106,000 median. Age-group means carry the same upward pull.

A second mistake is mixing years. A 2022 table and a 2025 table look alike, but values, populations and dollar bases differ. Many popular articles still show 2022 survey figures without saying so.

### Ignoring the Dollar Basis

The Fed restates older survey values in 2025 dollars, so the 2016 to 2025 table compares like with like after inflation. A table that mixes nominal dollars from different years can exaggerate or understate growth. Checking the dollar basis is a quick way to spot an unreliable comparison.

### Adding Unrelated Sources

Some tables combine 401(k) averages from a plan provider with IRA figures from a survey and present the total as household savings. The two cover different people. A sound retirement savings by age comparison uses one source, one population and one measure throughout.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is the Average Retirement Savings by Age in 2025?

Among families holding these accounts, SCF means were $48,400 under 35, $182,400 at 35 to 44, $415,800 at 45 to 54 and $670,200 at 55 to 64.

### What Is the Median Retirement Account Balance?

The SCF's conditional median for all families holding retirement accounts was $106,000 in 2025, in 2025 dollars. The mean was $451,100, pulled up by large accounts.

### What Share of Families Have Retirement Accounts?

Retirement accounts were held by 54.9% of families in 2025. Counting pensions too, 65.1% of families with a reference person aged 64 or younger participated in a plan.

### Why Did the Under-35 Average Fall?

The SCF samples different families each wave. New young savers joining plans with small balances can lower the average even when more young families are saving.

### Are These Numbers Targets I Should Hit?

No. They describe families, not goals. A suitable balance depends on expected spending, other income, pensions, debts and planned retirement age, which differ widely between households.
