# APY Calculator: Calculate Annual Percentage Yield

This APY calculator converts a stated annual interest rate into **annual percentage yield**, showing the effect of compounding over one year. Enter a rate and a compounding schedule, and add a balance and time period to see the interest in dollars.

```calculator
apy
```

Two deposit accounts can quote the same interest rate and still produce slightly different annual returns if they compound interest at different frequencies. APY folds that effect into one annual percentage, which makes accounts directly comparable. The sections below explain the formula, the assumptions, worked examples, and the limits readers should keep in mind when they use an APY calculator.

## Key Takeaways

- APY measures annual deposit yield after accounting for compounding.
- The same nominal interest rate can produce different APYs when compounding frequency changes.
- A 4.00% nominal rate compounded monthly produces about 4.074% APY.
- The CFPB's Regulation DD sets the rules institutions use to calculate and disclose APY.
- APY calculations assume principal and earned interest stay on deposit for the calculation period.
- Variable-rate savings accounts can change their APY after the account is opened.
- A calculator helps compare yields, but fees, rate conditions, balance rules, and withdrawal limits still affect value.

## How the APY Formula Works

The standard compounding formula shows why APY can exceed the nominal interest rate:

**APY = (1 + r ÷ n)^n − 1**

In this formula, r is the nominal annual interest rate as a decimal and n is the number of compounding periods per year. If a bank pays a 4.00% annual rate and compounds monthly, each month's interest is credited to the account and becomes part of the balance used for later interest.

The calculation is (1 + 0.04 ÷ 12)^12 − 1, which gives about **4.074%**. The difference looks small because it covers only one year. The purpose of APY is not to make compounding look dramatic; it is to give accounts that compound differently one consistent annual comparison.

## How Compounding Frequency Changes APY

A useful way to see the calculation is to hold the interest rate constant and change only the compounding frequency. The table assumes a **4.00% nominal annual rate** and a **$10,000 opening balance** left untouched for one year.

| Compounding Frequency | Approximate APY | Interest After One Year | Ending Balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Annually | 4.000% | $400.00 | $10,400.00 |
| Semiannually | 4.040% | $404.00 | $10,404.00 |
| Quarterly | 4.060% | $406.04 | $10,406.04 |
| Monthly | 4.074% | $407.42 | $10,407.42 |
| Daily | 4.081% | $408.08 | $10,408.08 |

These are Fin Loom calculations. Every row uses the same nominal rate; the only thing that changes is how often interest compounds. The APY calculator above reproduces each row when you pick the matching compounding frequency.

## The CFPB Uses a Regulatory APY Formula for Deposit Disclosures

The consumer formula above converts a nominal annual rate into an effective annual yield. For regulated deposit disclosures, the [CFPB's Regulation DD Appendix A](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/a/) sets out the formal method institutions use.

The [Regulation DD definitions](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/2/) describe APY as a percentage rate reflecting the total interest paid on an account, based on the interest rate and the frequency of compounding for a 365-day period. For accounts without a stated maturity, such as typical savings accounts, Appendix A assumes a 365-day term. It also assumes that all principal and interest stay on deposit for the entire term.

That regulatory framework is why a bank's disclosed APY should be treated as the authoritative account yield rather than a consumer's own calculation.

## How to Calculate APY From a 5% Interest Rate

A second example shows how the gap widens slightly as the nominal rate rises.

### Monthly Compounding at 5%

For a **5.00% nominal rate compounded monthly**, (1 + 0.05 ÷ 12)^12 − 1 gives about **5.116% APY**. A $10,000 balance would grow to about **$10,511.62** after one year if the rate stayed unchanged and the balance was left untouched. These are Fin Loom calculations that the APY calculator above reproduces.

### Daily Compounding at 5%

For the same 5.00% rate compounded daily, the APY is about **5.127%**, producing an ending balance near **$10,512.67**. The difference between monthly and daily compounding is about a dollar on $10,000. The bigger value of an APY calculator is that it makes accounts with different compounding structures directly comparable.

## APY and Interest Rate Should Not Be Used Interchangeably

The nominal interest rate and APY answer related but different questions. The interest rate tells you the annual rate applied before intra-year compounding is reflected. APY shows the resulting annual yield after compounding.

That is why an account can advertise a 4.00% interest rate and a 4.07% APY without the figures contradicting each other. The relationship is covered more fully in [APY vs interest rate](/money-banking/apy-vs-interest-rate/), which compares the same nominal rate across several compounding schedules.

## APY Is Not the Same as APR

APY is commonly used for interest-bearing deposit accounts. APR is commonly used for borrowing.

The CFPB explains that a loan's APR measures the interest rate plus additional fees charged with the loan. Deposit APY, meanwhile, reflects interest and compounding under Regulation DD. An APY calculator should therefore not be used as a loan APR calculator, because the two measures solve different disclosure problems.

## Calculating Dollar Interest From APY Is a Different Question

If you already know an account's APY, you do not need to work backward to its nominal rate to estimate a simple one-year return. For a balance that stays constant for the full year, a rough illustration is:

**Interest = Principal × APY**

A $25,000 balance at 4.50% APY would produce about $25,000 × 0.045 = **$1,125** in a year. That is an illustration, not a guarantee. Actual earnings can differ because the balance may change, the APY may be variable, fees may apply, or account conditions may affect the advertised yield. For balances that grow with regular deposits, a savings interest calculator that works month by month from an APY gives a closer estimate.

## Savings Accounts, Money Market Accounts, and CDs Use APY Differently

APY appears across several deposit products, but the account terms around it differ, and those terms decide how useful the yield is in practice.

### Savings Accounts

Savings-account APYs are commonly variable. The institution can change the rate when market conditions or its own deposit pricing changes, so the APY a calculator produces today describes the current rate rather than a fixed yield for the year ahead.

### Money Market Accounts

Bank and credit-union money market accounts are deposit products that pay interest while offering some transaction features. The CFPB notes they are insured by the FDIC or NCUA up to $250,000 per owner, and their APYs are also commonly variable.

### Certificates of Deposit

CDs typically offer a stated rate for a fixed term. Early withdrawal can trigger a penalty, so a higher APY does not automatically make a CD preferable to an accessible savings account. [APY meaning](/money-banking/apy-meaning/) explains these account types and the disclosure rules in more detail.

### Why the Calculator Treats Them the Same

The APY formula works the same way for each of these products. What differs is how long the rate lasts and how freely the money can move. The calculator answers the yield question; the account terms answer the access question, and both deserve a look before choosing.

## Why a Calculator Result Can Differ From Your Actual Bank Balance

An APY calculator is only as accurate as its assumptions. Your actual earnings can differ for several reasons:

- **Deposits:** Adding money during the year changes the balance that earns interest.
- **Withdrawals:** Taking money out reduces the interest-earning balance.
- **Variable Rates:** The account's rate can change after you calculate.
- **Promotional Rates:** An introductory APY may expire partway through the year.
- **Balance Tiers:** The rate may apply only to part of the balance.
- **Fees:** Charges reduce the balance and the effective return.
- **Crediting Rules:** A bank's interest-crediting practice can differ from the simplified example.

For regulated disclosures, the bank's stated APY is calculated under Regulation DD and should be treated as the official comparison figure.

## What to Compare After You Calculate APY

Yield is one part of a deposit-account decision. After comparing APYs, review monthly maintenance fees, minimum balance requirements, direct-deposit or activity requirements, whether the APY is fixed or variable, balance tiers or caps, withdrawal access, early-withdrawal penalties for CDs, and FDIC or NCUA insurance eligibility.

### Fees Can Reverse the Ranking

As an illustration, a 4.074% APY account with a $5 monthly fee earns about $407.42 on $10,000 in a year but pays $60 in fees, leaving $347.42. A fee-free account at 4.00% APY would leave $400.00. A slightly lower APY can win once costs are counted.

### Access Can Outweigh Yield

Money set aside for emergencies needs to be reachable quickly. A CD with a slightly higher APY can be the wrong home for that money if withdrawing early costs a penalty. Deciding what the money is for, a question [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) helps answer, comes before choosing the highest yield.

## An APY Calculator Is Best Used for Comparison

The main value of an APY calculator is not predicting every dollar of future interest. It converts different rate and compounding combinations into one annual measure, which makes deposit products easier to compare on a like-for-like basis.

The calculator should therefore sit alongside an account's disclosed APY, fees, conditions, and access rules rather than replace them. Used that way, an APY calculator answers its question well: which combination of rate and compounding produces the higher annual yield, under the stated assumptions.

## APY Calculator Results Over Several Years

An APY calculator can show more than one year, as long as the rate is assumed to stay fixed. That assumption is the main limit on any multi-year projection.

### Five and Ten Years at 4.074% APY

At the APY from a 4% rate compounded monthly, $10,000 left untouched grows to about $12,209.97 after five years and about $14,908.33 after ten. These are Fin Loom calculations, and the APY calculator reproduces them when you enter 4%, monthly, $10,000, and the matching number of years.

### Why Multi-Year Figures Are Illustrations

Most savings rates are variable, so a ten-year projection at today's rate is a scenario rather than a forecast. Running the calculator at a lower and a higher rate gives a sense of the range. For example, $10,000 grows to about $11,876.86 over five years at 3.5% APY and about $13,069.60 at 5.5% APY, both Fin Loom calculations. A two-point swing in the rate changes the five-year result by roughly $1,200, which shows how much any long projection depends on the rate assumption. For CDs, the fixed rate makes a projection more reliable, but only until the CD matures.

## APY for Terms Shorter Than a Year

The APY calculator above converts an annual rate, but Regulation DD also annualizes deposits that last less than a year. Appendix A uses the formula APY = 100 × [(1 + Interest ÷ Principal)^(365 ÷ Days in term) − 1].

### The CFPB's Six-Month CD Example

In Appendix A, a $1,000 six-month CD that pays $30.37 over 182 days has an APY of 6.18%. Annualizing lets a short CD be compared with a savings account on the same yearly basis, even though the CD's actual dollars of interest cover only half a year.

### What the Example Shows

The example is a reminder that APY is always yearly. A short-term deposit can carry an APY that looks similar to a savings account's, while paying interest for only part of the year. Checking the term alongside the APY shows how many dollars the deposit will produce before it matures. It also shows what happens next: at maturity the money can be withdrawn, renewed, or moved, and the new rate offered at that point may be higher or lower than the original APY. A short CD is a series of decisions rather than one, and each renewal is a natural point to run the APY calculator again with the new rate.

## How Interest Payouts Affect APY

An APY calculator assumes interest stays in the account and compounds. Regulation DD treats accounts that pay interest out differently, which is worth knowing before comparing results.

### When Interest Must Be Withdrawn

Appendix A normally assumes principal and interest stay on deposit for the term. That assumption is dropped if an account requires interest to be withdrawn, in which case the APY reflects the withdrawal requirement. Interest paid out instead of compounded earns nothing further, so the yield is lower than for the same rate left to compound.

### Long CDs That Pay Out Interest

For time accounts longer than a year that do not compound and must pay interest out at least annually, Appendix A lets the APY be disclosed as equal to the interest rate. Its example is a $1,000 two-year CD paying 6% by check every six months, which may show a 6.00% APY.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Do You Calculate APY?

Use APY = (1 + r ÷ n)^n − 1, where r is the nominal annual rate as a decimal and n is the number of compounding periods each year.

### Is APY Higher With Daily Compounding?

Usually, yes. At the same nominal rate, more frequent compounding gives a slightly higher APY because credited interest starts earning additional interest sooner during the year.

### What Is 5% Interest Compounded Monthly as APY?

A 5.00% nominal rate compounded monthly gives about 5.116% APY, assuming the rate stays constant and all interest remains in the account for the full year.

### Can I Calculate Savings Interest From APY?

Yes, for a simple one-year illustration. Multiply the opening balance by the APY, assuming the balance and rate stay unchanged and no fees, withdrawals, or deposits occur.

### Is a Bank's Disclosed APY More Accurate Than My Calculation?

For comparing regulated deposit accounts, use the disclosed APY. Banks calculate it under Regulation DD rules that can be more detailed than a simplified consumer formula or calculator.
