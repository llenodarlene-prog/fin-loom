# Buy the Dip Statistics: What History Can and Cannot Tell Investors

Buying the dip sounds sensible: pay less for an investment than someone paid a week earlier. The difficulty is that a lower price is not the same as a low price, and nobody knows in real time which decline will turn into a lasting recovery and which will keep falling.

The October and November 2026 market reversal shows that uncertainty clearly. Technology shares weakened through October, and the Nasdaq Composite then fell about 5.5% in a single session on November 5. A buyer after the first decline had no guarantee that another sharp day would not follow. This page separates what the market did from what a hypothetical investor might have done, states every assumption, and explains how to buy the dip as part of a plan rather than a guess. Data runs through the November 6, 2026 close.

## Key Takeaways

- **Declines varied by index:** in October 2026, the Nasdaq fell 5.3%, the S&P 500 fell 0.9% and the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 5.0%.
- **A dip can deepen:** the Nasdaq fell another 5.5% on November 5, 2026, after its October decline.
- **History shows recoveries and long slumps:** the S&P 500 compounded at about 10.0% a year from 1928 to 2025, but the 2000s ended slightly negative.
- **Dollar-cost averaging is different:** FINRA describes it as investing equal amounts at regular intervals regardless of market conditions.
- **Hindsight inflates results:** backtests that assume buying at the exact bottom use information no investor had at the time.

## What Does Buy the Dip Mean?

Buying the dip means purchasing an investment after its price has fallen, usually expecting the price to recover. It can describe adding to a diversified fund, buying a single company's shares, or trying to profit from a short-term rebound.

Those are very different risk decisions. A broad index has historically recovered over long periods, while an individual company may never return to its old price. Even an investment with a strong long-term outlook can fall further after the purchase.

### Dip Buying Versus Rebalancing

Rebalancing starts from a target mix, such as 70% stocks and 30% bonds. When stocks fall, the mix drifts, and rebalancing buys stocks to restore the target. Dip buying starts from a price move. The first follows a plan set in advance; the second reacts to the market.

### Dip Buying Versus Market Timing

Buying the dip is a form of market timing. It assumes the investor can tell when a price is low enough. In practice, a "dip" is only clear after prices recover, and a decline that looks like a dip may become a much longer fall.

### What These Figures Do Not Show

These numbers show that declines differed by index and date. They do not establish a success rate for dip buying, the best entry price or a reliable recovery time. Anyone quoting a "win rate" for buying dips needs to show the rules, the period and every losing case.

### The Dip After the Dip

Someone who bought the Nasdaq after its October decline faced a further 5.5% one-day fall on November 5. FinLoom's [stock market crash 2026 statistics](/markets-stocks/stock-market-crash-2026-statistics/) show that by November 5, the Nasdaq was 11.9% below its October 6 record close, deeper than its October monthly loss.

## Why Is Hindsight a Problem in Dip-Buying Statistics?

A chart shows an obvious bottom after the fact. On the day that bottom happened, investors could not know it would be the lowest price. Backtests that assume a purchase at the exact low benefit from information no real investor had.

Another common problem is studying only the dips that recovered. A complete study must also include declines that kept going, investments that never recovered, dividends, fees, taxes and a fixed rule for when the investor would buy and sell. Without that full set of cases, an advertised success rate is not evidence.

### Survivorship Bias

Indexes replace companies that shrink or fail with ones that grow. A study of dip buying in an index today looks only at the survivors. Many individual stocks that fell sharply in the past never recovered, but they no longer appear in current index data.

### Rules That Could Be Followed in Real Time

A useful dip-buying test needs a rule an investor could follow without knowing the future, such as "buy after a 10% fall from the last high." The results then depend on how many falls continued past 10%, which is exactly the uncertainty dip buyers face.

## A Transparent Example: Investing Now or Waiting

Consider a hypothetical fund priced at $100 a share. The price later falls to $90 and then returns to $100. These are invented prices for illustration, not 2026 market data.

| Strategy | Purchase | Shares bought with $900 | Value if price returns to $100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Invest now at $100 | $900 | 9 | $900 |
| Wait and buy at $90 | $900 | 10 | $1,000 |
| Stay in cash | $0 | 0 | $900 before interest |

Waiting works best here only because the $90 price arrived and the fund recovered. Change the path and the result changes. If the price falls from $90 to $70 instead, the ten shares are worth $700, a $200 loss on $900. If the price rises straight from $100 to $110 and never dips, the waiting investor buys nothing and misses the gain. None of the rows include trading costs or taxes.

### What the Example Shows

The table shows an arithmetic relationship, not a probability. A lower purchase price helps only if a lower price appears and the investment later recovers. The risk of waiting is that the dip never comes, or comes after a rise larger than the dip itself.

### The Cost of Waiting

Cash held while waiting usually earns less than stocks over long periods. If the market rises steadily for a year while an investor waits for a 10% dip, the eventual purchase may happen at a higher price than the original one, even after the dip.

## How Is Dollar-Cost Averaging Different?

[FINRA describes dollar-cost averaging](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/dollar-cost-averaging) as investing money in equal portions at regular intervals, regardless of market conditions. A fixed contribution buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high. Contributions from each paycheck into a workplace retirement plan are a common example.

FINRA notes that compared with investing everything at once, dollar-cost averaging can limit losses during a significant decline, because money not yet invested is unaffected. It does not prevent losses on money already invested, and it can mean missing gains if prices rise steadily.

### A Simple Dollar-Cost Averaging Example

Suppose an investor puts $300 a month into a fund for three months at prices of $100, $75 and $100. These are invented figures. They buy 3, 4 and 3 shares, ten shares in total for $900, an average cost of $90 a share. The average price over the three months was about $91.67, so steady buying produced a slightly lower average cost.

### Lump Sum Versus Gradual Investing

Someone with money arriving from each paycheck faces a different choice from someone with a large sum already available. The first can follow a contribution schedule. The second must weigh the cost of waiting against the risk and discomfort of investing all at once. Neither approach wins in every scenario.

## Which Risks Count Most Before Buying a Dip?

Near-term cash needs come first. Money needed for rent, emergencies or a purchase within a few years should not be treated like long-term investment money, because a dip can deepen and last longer than expected.

Concentration is the second risk. Buying more of a falling stock can increase exposure to the very problem causing its fall. FinLoom's comparison of [crypto vs stocks](/investing/crypto-vs-stocks/) shows how much deeper declines can run in more volatile assets, which raises the stakes of buying into a falling market.

### Questions to Ask First

Would this purchase keep my portfolio near its target mix, or push one holding much higher? Could I hold the investment through a further 20% fall without needing to sell? Am I buying a diversified fund or a single company with its own risks? Clear answers turn a reaction into a decision.

### Emotional Risk

Buying during a sharp fall feels uncomfortable, and that discomfort can lead to selling at a loss if prices keep falling. A plan written in calm conditions, stating when and how much to invest, helps investors avoid decisions made in the middle of a selloff.

## Taxes and Costs When You Buy the Dip

Trading costs and taxes reduce the benefit of any timing strategy. Each purchase may carry a commission or a bid-ask spread, and frequent buying and selling in a taxable account can trigger capital gains taxes. Those costs are easy to leave out of a backtest and hard to avoid in real life.

Inside a retirement account, buying and selling usually does not trigger tax each year, which removes one cost. Trading spreads still apply. For most long-term investors, the simpler and cheaper approach is regular contributions to a low-cost fund, with occasional rebalancing, rather than frequent attempts to buy the dip.

### A Cost Example

Suppose an investor makes 20 extra trades a year, each costing $5 in spreads. That is $100 a year in trading costs, before any taxes. These figures are illustrative. On a modest account, those costs can easily outweigh the benefit of buying a little lower now and then.

### Why Simple Approaches Often Win on Cost

Regular contributions on a fixed schedule involve few decisions and few trades. That keeps costs low and removes the need to judge whether a dip is real. It does not guarantee better returns, but it avoids the hidden costs that often erode the results of frequent dip buying.

## What Can History Tell Investors?

History shows that markets have suffered large declines and later recovered, but timing and depth vary widely. Damodaran's long-run dataset records an S&P 500 total return of -36.55% in 2008 and +18.02% in 2020, a year that included a severe crash. Those figures show why the holding period is important, not that every purchase after a decline is safe.

By our calculation from [Damodaran's historical returns dataset](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html), the S&P 500 compounded at about 10.0% a year from 1928 to 2025. Yet the 2000 to 2009 decade ended with a compound return of about -0.95% a year. An investor who bought a dip early in that decade waited years to see a lasting gain.

### Long Averages Hide Long Waits

A 10% long-run average does not mean 10% every year or every decade. FinLoom's [stock market return by year](/markets-stocks/stock-market-return-by-year/) table shows years ranging from -36.55% to +32.15% since 2008 alone, and two decades since 1930 with slightly negative compound returns.

### Past Returns Are Not a Forecast

Historical data frames what is possible. It cannot identify the next bottom, the length of the next recovery or whether a particular dip is a buying opportunity. Any decision to buy the dip should rest on a plan and a time horizon, not on the assumption that the past will repeat.

## How to Build a Dip Rule Into a Plan

For investors who want to buy the dip in a disciplined way, the safest approach ties purchases to a plan rather than to headlines. The steps below are general, not personal advice.

### Set the Target First

Decide on a target mix of stocks, bonds and cash based on your goals and time horizon. Then decide how far a holding can drift before you act, for example five percentage points. When a fall in stocks pushes the mix past that limit, buying stocks to restore the target is rebalancing, not guessing. The five-point figure is only an example.

### Decide the Amount in Advance

Some investors keep a small, separate cash reserve for buying during declines, alongside their regular contributions. Deciding the amount and the trigger ahead of time, and writing both down, makes it easier to act calmly and limits the damage if a dip keeps falling.

## What the November 2026 Selloff Shows About Timing

The early November 2026 sessions illustrate the problem in miniature. FRED's Nasdaq Composite closes show a rise to 25,736.51 on November 4, a 5.5% drop to 24,315.00 on November 5 and a partial rebound to 24,579.42 on November 6. An investor who bought on November 4 after October's decline was hit the next day. One who bought on November 5 had a small gain by November 6.

Neither outcome could have been known in advance, and three sessions say nothing about the weeks ahead. The point is not which day was best, but that a dip can look like a bargain one day and deepen the next.

## Common Mistakes When Trying to Buy the Dip

The first mistake is assuming every decline is a dip. Some are the start of longer falls. The second is trusting win rates that leave out losing cases or assume perfect timing. The third is using money needed soon.

The fourth is buying more of one falling stock without checking concentration. The fifth is confusing dip buying with dollar-cost averaging. One reacts to price moves; the other invests on a fixed schedule. Knowing which you are doing makes it easier to buy the dip, or decline to, for the right reasons.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Buying the Dip Guaranteed to Work?

No. A lower purchase price helps only if the investment later recovers. Prices can keep falling after a purchase, as the Nasdaq did on November 5, 2026.

### Is Buying the Dip the Same as Dollar-Cost Averaging?

No. Dip buying responds to a price decline. Dollar-cost averaging invests equal amounts at regular intervals regardless of price, as FINRA describes.

### Can Historical Charts Identify the Next Bottom?

No. A bottom is only known in hindsight. Investors in real time face uncertain prices, changing news and the possibility of further declines.

### Should I Buy the Dip in 2026?

This page cannot answer that. It depends on your goals, time horizon, cash needs and holdings. A plan set in advance helps more than reacting to headlines.

### What Is a Buy the Dip Strategy?

It is a rule for buying after prices fall, such as investing after a set percentage decline. Its results depend on how often declines continue past the trigger.
