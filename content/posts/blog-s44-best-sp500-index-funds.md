# Best S&P 500 Index Funds for 2026: Four Low-Cost Choices Compared

Choosing the best S&P 500 index fund is less about predicting which provider will win and more about picking a low-friction way to own the same large U.S. companies. The funds below follow the same benchmark, but their structures, expense ratios and buying mechanics differ. Those differences show up when you invest small amounts each month, move a retirement account, or trade often.

The four funds here are not ranked by recent returns. A fund that closely tracks an index cannot keep beating that index just because its brand is better known. FinLoom compares cost, access and practical use instead, using fees checked on each issuer's own materials in late October and early November 2026. None of this is a recommendation to buy any fund.

## Key Takeaways

- FXAIX is a Fidelity index mutual fund with a 0.015% expense ratio and no minimum initial investment.
- VOO and IVV are ETFs that each list a 0.03% expense ratio, which makes them close substitutes for most buyers.
- SPYM is State Street's low-cost S&P 500 ETF at 0.02%, and it is a different fund from SPY, which costs 0.0945%.
- On a $10,000 balance, the gap between 0.02% and 0.03% is about $1 a year, so account fees and convenience can outweigh it.
- Every fund here is concentrated: the top 10 holdings made up 39.03% of FXAIX's net assets on September 30, 2026.

## How We Picked the Best S&P 500 Index Fund Options

All four funds track the same index, so the useful differences are practical. We focused on four questions.

### Cost and Structure

We compared each fund's published expense ratio and whether it is a mutual fund or an ETF. A mutual fund trades once a day at its net asset value, while an ETF trades during market hours at a market price. That difference changes how a monthly investing plan works more than a basis point of cost does.

### Access, Minimums and Portability

We noted minimum investments and how easily each fund can be bought and moved between brokers. A fund that is cheap but hard to buy at your broker, or awkward to transfer later, can cost more in time and fees than a slightly pricier fund that fits the account you already use.

## Fidelity 500 Index Fund (FXAIX): Strongest for a Simple Mutual Fund Routine

[Fidelity's FXAIX fact sheet](https://institutional.fidelity.com/app/proxy/content?literatureURL=%2F770893.PDF), dated September 30, 2026, lists a 0.015% expense ratio and a $0 minimum initial investment. The fund launched on February 17, 1988 and held 508 positions on that date.

The zero minimum makes FXAIX useful for investors who buy a dollar amount every payday instead of deciding how many shares to buy. Mutual funds trade at a single end-of-day price, which can make the process less distracting than watching ETF quotes move during the day.

That convenience has a boundary. The natural home for FXAIX is an account with easy access to Fidelity mutual funds. Someone who plans to change brokers later should check whether the fund can transfer in kind or would need to be sold. The S&P 500 exposure itself is the same as in the other three funds.

FXAIX's fact sheet also reports a three-year tracking error of 0.01, a measure of how closely the fund's returns have followed the index. For an index fund, a low number is the goal, because the whole point is to match the benchmark rather than beat it.

## Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO): A Familiar ETF for Long-Term Investors

VOO tracks the S&P 500 and listed a 0.03% expense ratio and a $1 minimum when FinLoom checked Vanguard's VOO page on October 26, 2026, for our earlier comparison of index funds and mutual funds. Vanguard describes VOO as an exchange-traded share class of its 500 Index Fund.

Because VOO trades on an exchange throughout the day, the price you pay can differ slightly from the fund's net asset value. For investors who already use a brokerage account, its plain benchmark objective makes it an uncomplicated core U.S. stock holding.

Being an ETF is not automatically better than being a mutual fund. Some brokers let you buy fractional ETF shares on a schedule, but those features vary by account. If your plan is monthly and long-term, a broker that carries out the plan reliably can count for more than the ticker you pick.

## iShares Core S&P 500 ETF (IVV): An Equally Plausible Core ETF

[BlackRock's IVV page](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239726/ishares-core-sp-500-etf) lists a 0.03% expense ratio and net assets of about $850 billion as of November 5, 2026. The fund launched on May 15, 2000 and tracks the S&P 500 Index.

For an ordinary buy-and-hold investor, that cost makes IVV economically close to VOO. The better question is not whether IVV will beat VOO next quarter, but whether your broker supports the trade sizes, automatic investments and account types you need.

Both funds hold the same large U.S. companies and both fall when U.S. stocks fall. The choice between them mostly comes down to which one your broker makes easier to buy on a schedule, and which one you already hold in other accounts.

## SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 ETF (SPYM): The Lowest ETF Fee in This Shortlist

State Street's SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 ETF trades under the ticker SPYM and lists a 0.02% gross expense ratio. Its fund page showed a net asset value of $86.28 as of November 5, 2026, and an inception date of November 8, 2005.

SPYM is easy to confuse with SPY, State Street's original S&P 500 ETF. [State Street's comparison of SPY and SPYM](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/intermediary/insights/comparing-spy-and-spym-two-state-street-etfs-for-s-p-500-exposure) uses a 0.0945% expense ratio for SPY and 0.02% for SPYM in its cost examples. Both target the same index, but State Street positions SPY for frequent trading and SPYM for low-cost long-term ownership.

That distinction is easy to miss because the tickers look alike. A reader working from an older article could buy the wrong fund by mistake. Always confirm the fund name, ticker and issuer before placing an order.

## What a Small Expense Ratio Difference Costs

Fee gaps between these funds are small in dollar terms. The table below shows yearly fund expenses at a constant balance for illustration. Real balances move, so these figures are illustrations, not forecasts.

| Balance | 0.015% | 0.02% | 0.03% | 0.0945% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10,000 | $1.50 | $2.00 | $3.00 | $9.45 |
| $25,000 | $3.75 | $5.00 | $7.50 | $23.63 |
| $100,000 | $15.00 | $20.00 | $30.00 | $94.50 |

The gap between 0.02% and 0.03% on $25,000 is $2.50 a year, not $250. That does not make fees irrelevant. Costs compound over decades, especially on large balances, and the SPY column shows how a trading-oriented fund costs several times more to hold.

It does mean a one-basis-point saving should be weighed against operating costs: your broker's transaction fees, fractional-share support, automatic investing tools, and whether you can move assets easily later.

## ETF or Index Mutual Fund: Which Works Better?

An ETF trades like a stock during market hours, while a mutual fund generally prices once each trading day. Both can follow the S&P 500. For a beginner placing monthly orders, a mutual fund may feel simpler because dollars go in without a separate trading decision.

An ETF can be easier to move between brokers, although fractional shares and broker-specific services may not transfer the same way as whole shares. FinLoom's guide to the [best investment apps](/investing/best-investment-apps/) compares which brokers support fractional shares and recurring buys.

## The Risk All Four Share

The S&P 500 is weighted by market value, so the biggest companies have the largest effect on returns. Fidelity's FXAIX fact sheet shows how concentrated that makes the index: on September 30, 2026, the top 10 holdings made up 39.03% of the fund's net assets, the top 20 made up 49.90%, and information technology alone was 39.68%.

The top 10 holdings on that date were Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet's two share classes, Broadcom, Meta Platforms, Micron Technology and Tesla. A sharp fall in a few of those businesses moves the whole fund.

That is different from owning the whole U.S. market or a globally diversified portfolio. FXAIX held 99.58% U.S. stocks on the fact sheet date, so it carries no meaningful international exposure.

Owning several S&P 500 funds adds almost no diversification, because their holdings overlap almost completely. One fund can serve as the U.S. large-company core of a portfolio, but cash needs, bonds and international stocks still need their own place.

## How S&P 500 Funds Fit Retirement Accounts

Many investors hold an S&P 500 fund inside an IRA. In a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals are tax-free, so any of these funds can grow without yearly tax on dividends or gains. The fund choice itself does not change the account's tax rules.

## How to Check a Fund Before You Buy

Whichever fund looks like the best S&P 500 index fund for you, a few minutes with the issuer's own documents can prevent costly mix-ups. Fund names, tickers and costs change over time, and articles, including this one, can fall out of date. These checks take little time and use free information that every issuer publishes.

### Confirm the Ticker, Issuer and Index

Start with the basics. Check that the ticker, full fund name and issuer match what you intend to buy, and that the benchmark listed on the fund page is the S&P 500 Index. This is how you avoid buying SPY when you meant SPYM, or a sector fund with a similar name when you meant a broad index fund. It also helps to check whether the fund is a mutual fund or an ETF, because that decides whether you buy at the end-of-day price or at a market price during the day, and whether your broker can schedule the purchase automatically.

### Read the Fact Sheet Numbers

A fund's fact sheet shows the expense ratio, minimum investment, number of holdings, top 10 weight and tracking error, usually with a date. Compare those numbers with the fund page and note the date. An older fact sheet may not reflect a recent fee cut, ticker change or shift in the index's largest companies.

Finally, look at your own broker's fee schedule. A best S&P 500 index fund on paper can cost more in practice if your broker charges to buy it, does not support recurring purchases, or will not let you buy fractional shares. The cheapest fund is the one that fits the account you already have.

## How to Make a Choice That Lasts

There is no reason to own all four funds. If you already use Fidelity, FXAIX's zero minimum and daily pricing make a monthly plan easy to keep up. If you want an ETF you can hold at almost any broker, VOO, IVV and SPYM are close substitutes, and purchase costs or account features can break a tie.

Rather than switching funds over a tiny fee gap each year, set a contribution plan and review it when your broker, tax situation or goals change. Sticking with a sound routine usually does more than fine-tuning four near-identical funds. Choosing the best S&P 500 index fund for you comes down to the account you use and how you plan to buy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is the Best S&P 500 Index Fund for Low Costs?

Among these four, FXAIX has the lowest published expense ratio at 0.015%. Your total cost can still differ once account fees, trading costs and broker features are included.

### Is SPYM the Same as SPY?

No. Both track the S&P 500, but they are separate State Street funds. SPYM costs 0.02% a year and SPY 0.0945%, so confirm the ticker before ordering.

### Is VOO Better Than IVV?

Neither has a lasting return edge, since both track the same index at 0.03%. Compare broker access, trading convenience and spreads rather than short-term performance between the two funds.

### Does the S&P 500 Cover the Whole Stock Market?

No. It tracks about 500 large U.S. companies and leaves out smaller U.S. companies and foreign markets. A total-market or global fund gives broader coverage than any S&P 500 fund.

### Can I Invest Monthly in an S&P 500 ETF?

Often yes, but recurring purchases and fractional shares vary by broker. Check those features before choosing an ETF for a small automated plan, or consider a mutual fund instead.

### Why Is the Best S&P 500 Index Fund Not the One With the Best Returns?

Funds tracking the same index return almost the same amount before fees. Small return gaps mostly reflect cost and tracking differences, so past returns are a poor way to choose.
