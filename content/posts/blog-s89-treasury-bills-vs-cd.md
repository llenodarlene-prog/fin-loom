# Treasury Bills vs CD: Rates, Pricing, Taxes and Access Compared

Treasury bills and certificates of deposit both hold cash for a set period and pay a fixed return. That makes the treasury bills vs cd decision look like a simple rate comparison. It is not quite that simple. The two are quoted differently, taxed differently, protected differently and handled differently if you need the money early.

This guide compares them using primary sources. TreasuryDirect explains how bills are sold and priced. The FDIC publishes national average deposit rates. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED) posts daily bill rates. Figures are dated, and examples are labeled. Sources were reviewed on November 21, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Bills are sold at a discount:** TreasuryDirect says bills sell at a discount or at face value, and you get face value at maturity.
- **Terms run four to 52 weeks:** bills come in 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52-week terms, with a $100 minimum.
- **No state or local tax on bills:** TreasuryDirect lists federal tax on bill interest but no state or local tax.
- **National CD averages trail bill rates:** the FDIC's November 16, 2026 figures show 1.40% for a 6-month CD, while FRED shows the 6-month bill at 4.31% on November 19.
- **Quotes differ:** bills use a discount rate on a 360-day basis, while CDs usually quote an annual percentage yield (APY).
- **Protection differs:** CDs at insured banks are FDIC deposits, while the FDIC lists Treasury bills among products it does not insure.

## What Is the Difference Between Treasury Bills vs CD?

In any treasury bills vs cd comparison, a Treasury bill is the short-term loan to the U.S. government. [TreasuryDirect's Treasury bill page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/) says bills are sold for terms from four weeks to 52 weeks. They are sold at a discount or at par, and when a bill matures, you are paid its face value. The gap between the price and face value is your interest.

A CD is a time deposit at a bank or credit union. You deposit money for a set term and earn a stated rate. The FDIC lists certificates of deposit among the deposit products it insures. If you withdraw early, the bank usually charges a penalty set in the account terms.

### How Each One Pays

Bills pay nothing along the way. You pay less than face value up front and receive the full face value at maturity. TreasuryDirect describes interest on a bill as the difference between what you paid and the face value you receive when the bill matures. There are no coupon payments.

CDs usually pay interest at the rate in the account agreement. Depending on the bank, interest may compound and be credited monthly, quarterly or at maturity. The APY reflects compounding over a year, which makes it easier to compare CDs with each other. It does not line up directly with a bill's discount rate.

### Who Backs Each One

Bills are obligations of the U.S. Treasury. CDs are bank deposits. The FDIC says it insures deposits, including CDs, up to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each insured bank.

The FDIC's list of products it does not insure includes U.S. Treasury bills, bonds and notes. That is because Treasury securities are not deposits. Their safety comes from the government that issues them, not from deposit insurance. Readers comparing the two should know that the protection comes from different sources.

## Treasury Bills vs CD Side by Side

The table below summarizes the main features. Treasury facts come from TreasuryDirect, insurance facts from the FDIC, and the CD features describe common terms rather than any specific bank's offer.

| Feature | Treasury Bill | Bank CD |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | U.S. Treasury | Bank or credit union |
| Terms | 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52 weeks | Set by the bank, often months to years |
| Minimum | $100, in $100 increments | Set by the bank |
| How interest works | Bought at a discount, paid face value at maturity | Stated rate, usually quoted as APY |
| Federal income tax | Yes | Yes |
| State and local income tax | No | Generally yes |
| Protection | Backed by the U.S. Treasury; not FDIC insured | FDIC insured at insured banks, up to limits |
| Before maturity | Can be sold | Usually an early withdrawal penalty |

In a treasury bills vs cd comparison, these rows cover nearly every practical difference. The rows below each heading show where the products agree and where they part ways, so a saver can focus on the few that change the outcome.

### What the Table Highlights

Three rows tend to decide the comparison: rate, tax and access. The rate is where most people start. Tax treatment can change the after-tax result, especially in states with income tax. Access shapes what happens if plans change before the term ends.

Terms also differ in shape. Bills top out at 52 weeks, so they suit short horizons. CDs extend further, often several years, which lets savers lock a rate for longer. For a horizon over a year, a bill would need to be rolled into a new one at whatever rate is available then.

### Buying Each One

TreasuryDirect says bills are issued in electronic form only. You can buy them through TreasuryDirect or through a bank, broker or dealer. Bills of most terms are auctioned weekly, and 52-week bills every four weeks. The rate is fixed at auction.

CDs are bought directly from a bank or credit union, or through a brokerage that offers brokered CDs. Rates are set by each institution and change over time. Comparing several offers is common, since rates vary widely from one bank to the next.

### Reading the Rate Table

The national CD averages sit well below bill rates in this snapshot. The FDIC's figures are averages across many institutions, so they include banks paying very little. Some banks and credit unions offer CDs far above the national average, so an individual offer can look very different from these numbers.

The two columns are also measured differently. The FDIC rates are deposit rates, while the FRED bill rates are on a discount basis. A discount rate understates the bill's true yield somewhat, because it is calculated on face value rather than the price paid and uses a 360-day year. The gap in this table is large enough that the measurement difference does not close it.

### Why Rates Move

Bill rates change with every auction and every trading day, and CD rates change whenever banks reset them. The FDIC's national rate cap figures are published monthly, on the third Monday. Any comparison is a snapshot, which is why the dates sit in the table headers.

Inflation is one force behind short-term rates. When inflation runs hot, short-term rates often rise with it, and when it cools, they can fall. The [CPI vs PPI vs PCE](/money-banking/cpi-vs-ppi-vs-pce/) guide explains how the main inflation measures differ, which helps put rate moves in context.

## How Treasury Bill Pricing Works

TreasuryDirect publishes the pricing formula. Its page on [understanding pricing](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/understanding-pricing/) gives the price as face value times one minus the discount rate times time, divided by 360. Time is the number of days to maturity. A 26-week bill has 182 days.

TreasuryDirect's own example uses a $1,000 26-week bill sold at a 0.145% discount rate. The formula gives a price of $999.27, so the buyer earns $0.73 when the bill pays $1,000 at maturity. That example comes from a period of very low rates, so it shows the method, not today's yield.

### A Worked Example at a Current Rate

Here is the same formula with a more recent rate. Assume a $10,000 26-week bill at a 4.31% discount rate, matching FRED's 6-month bill rate on November 19, 2026. The price is $10,000 times one minus 0.0431 times 182 divided by 360, which works out to $9,782.11.

At maturity, the buyer receives $10,000. The difference, $217.89, is the interest. Actual auction results set the real price, and secondary market prices differ from the daily FRED average. The example shows the mechanics, not a quote. The [treasury bill calculator](/investing/treasury-bill-calculator/) runs the same formula for any face value, rate and term.

### Comparing a Bill With a CD

A CD example shows why the quote type counts. Assume $10,000 in a 6-month CD paying the FDIC national average of 1.40% APY. Over half a year, it would earn about $69.76, assuming the rate compounds as an annual yield. That is far below the bill's $217.89 in this snapshot.

The comparison changes when a specific CD pays far more than the national average. A CD offer close to bill rates would make the comparison closer, and then taxes and access would carry more weight. Running both numbers with the actual offers in front of you gives the clearest answer.

## How Taxes Change the Comparison

In treasury bills vs cd decisions, tax treatment is a real difference. TreasuryDirect's bill page lists federal tax due on interest earned and no state or local taxes. CD interest is generally taxable at the federal, state and local levels. For savers in states with income tax, that difference raises a bill's after-tax return compared with a CD at the same pre-tax rate.

Federal tax applies to both. Because a bill pays its interest as one amount at maturity, while a CD may credit interest along the way, the timing of taxable interest can differ. IRS guidance on interest income covers the reporting details.

### Estimating the State Tax Effect

Here is a simple illustration. Suppose a saver pays a 5% state income tax rate and earns $217.89 of interest. If that interest came from a CD, about $10.89 would go to state tax. If it came from a bill, no state tax would apply. Federal tax would apply in both cases.

The effect is larger in high-tax states and zero in states with no income tax. It also grows with the size of the holding. For a saver in a high-tax state comparing a CD and a bill at similar pre-tax rates, the state exemption can tip the result. State rules vary, so checking your own state's treatment is a sensible step.

### Taxes in Retirement Accounts

Inside a tax-advantaged retirement account, the state tax advantage of bills generally does not come into play, because the account's own tax rules apply. Some brokerage retirement accounts allow both bills and brokered CDs. In that setting, rate and access may count for more than tax.

That makes account type part of the treasury bills vs cd decision. A taxable account in a high-tax state is where the bill's exemption helps most. In other settings, the comparison may come down mostly to rate and flexibility.

## Access Before Maturity

What happens if you need the money early? TreasuryDirect says you can hold a bill until it matures or sell it before it matures. Selling means accepting the market price that day, which can be higher or lower than what you paid, depending on how rates have moved.

A CD usually carries an early withdrawal penalty. The size of the penalty depends on the bank and the term. Some banks offer no-penalty CDs at lower rates. Reading the penalty terms before opening a CD shows what early access would cost.

### Brokered CDs and Bills at a Brokerage

Brokered CDs sit in between. They are bought through a brokerage, and instead of paying a penalty, you can try to sell them on the secondary market. Like a bill sold early, the price depends on market rates, and there may be no buyer at a good price.

Bills held at a brokerage can be sold during market hours. For savers who value quick access, where the bill is held is part of the decision.

### Matching the Term to the Need

The simplest way to avoid early access costs is to match the term to the date you need the money. A bill or CD maturing just before a known expense avoids both selling and penalties. That planning step often makes the access question less important than it first appears.

This connects to a wider point about short-term money. Cash needed within a set period usually belongs in stable, predictable places rather than in investments that can fall. The [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) guide covers how to think about that split across goals.

## The Bottom Line

The treasury bills vs cd choice comes down to rate, tax and access. In the November 2026 snapshot, national average CD rates sit well below Treasury bill rates, though individual CDs can pay far more than average. Bills avoid state and local income tax, use a discount price and can be sold early. CDs offer FDIC insurance at insured banks and longer terms, usually with an early withdrawal penalty. Compare the actual offers, after tax, for your own timeline.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are Treasury Bills Safer Than CDs?

They are protected differently. Bills are backed by the U.S. Treasury, while CDs at insured banks carry FDIC deposit insurance up to limits. The FDIC does not insure bills.

### Do I Pay State Tax on Treasury Bill Interest?

No. TreasuryDirect lists federal tax due on bill interest and no state or local taxes. CD interest is generally taxable at federal, state and local levels.

### What Is the Minimum to Buy a Treasury Bill?

TreasuryDirect lists a $100 minimum purchase, in $100 increments. Bills are issued in electronic form only, in terms from four weeks to 52 weeks.

### Can I Get My Money Out of a Treasury Bill Early?

Yes, by selling it before maturity. The price depends on market rates that day. A CD usually charges an early withdrawal penalty instead, based on the bank's terms.

### Why Do CD Averages Look So Low Compared With Bills?

The FDIC's national averages include many banks paying low rates. Individual CDs can pay much more. Bill rates use a discount basis, which differs from a CD's APY.
