# Stock Research Analyst: What the Role Does and How the Work Gets Used

A stock research analyst turns company information into an investment view. The work begins with financial statements, industry data, management commentary, market conditions, and competitive research, then moves into forecasts, valuation, risk analysis, and written conclusions.

The title can describe different jobs. Sell-side analysts publish research for brokerage clients and institutional investors. Buy-side analysts work inside asset managers, hedge funds, pension funds, or other investment organizations and support internal portfolio decisions. Both roles can involve deep company research, but the audience, incentives, and level of public visibility differ.

Current U.S. labor data show that analytical finance remains a sizable profession. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a **$102,740 median annual wage for financial and investment analysts in May 2025** and projected employment in that category to grow **7.2% from 2024 to 2034**, from about 377,200 jobs to 404,300. Research analysts are not a separate BLS occupational category, so those figures should be treated as the closest broad benchmark rather than a salary guarantee for equity research specifically.

## Key Takeaways

- Stock research analysts collect company and industry data, build financial models, forecast results, estimate value, and communicate investment conclusions.
- CFA Institute separates research roles broadly into **equity research** and **credit research**, with buy-side and sell-side career paths inside both.
- Sell-side analysts typically publish research for clients, while buy-side analysts use research internally to support portfolio decisions.
- BLS reported **$102,740 median annual pay** for financial and investment analysts in May 2025.
- BLS projects employment for financial and investment analysts to grow **7.2% from 2024 to 2034**.
- Financial modeling, accounting, valuation, industry research, data analysis, and written communication are core skills.
- The CFA Program can be relevant to the career, and CFA Level I and II passers may qualify for an exemption from Part I of FINRA's Series 86 research analyst exam.
- Good research separates facts, assumptions, forecasts, and valuation rather than presenting one target price as certainty.

## What a Stock Research Analyst Actually Does

CFA Institute describes research analysts as professionals who collect data about investable companies, analyze the material, build earnings models, conduct valuations, and communicate recommendations to portfolio managers or clients.

That description captures the central workflow. Analysts are not paid simply to summarize earnings releases. Their value comes from deciding which information matters, converting operating data into forecasts, testing assumptions, and explaining why the market may be underestimating or overestimating a company's future results.

A research process often includes:

- reading SEC filings and earnings releases;
- following company management and investor presentations;
- studying industry structure and competitors;
- building revenue, margin, cash-flow, and earnings forecasts;
- comparing valuation methods;
- identifying catalysts and risks;
- updating models when new information arrives;
- and communicating a conclusion through reports, meetings, or investment committees.

## Buy-Side and Sell-Side Analysts Do Similar Work for Different Audiences

The terms buy-side and sell-side describe where research sits in the investment ecosystem. Both analysts may cover the same company and use similar financial statements, but the audience and purpose of the work differ.

| Area | Sell-Side Research | Buy-Side Research |
|---|---|---|
| Primary audience | External clients and institutional investors | Internal portfolio managers and investment teams |
| Typical employer | Investment bank or brokerage | Asset manager, hedge fund, pension, family office |
| Research output | Published notes, models, ratings, target prices | Internal memos, models, position recommendations |
| Coverage | Often a defined sector or company group | Can be broader depending on fund mandate |
| Public visibility | Higher | Usually lower |
| Main decision supported | Client investment views and trading interest | Portfolio allocation and position sizing |

CFA Institute notes that sell-side equity analysts often cover fewer stocks but in greater depth, while buy-side analysts may work across broader investment mandates depending on the organization.

## The Research Process Starts Before the Spreadsheet

Analysts eventually spend substantial time in models, but the quality of the model depends on the quality of the research that enters it.

Industry structure matters because a 10% revenue growth assumption means something different in a mature utility business than in a rapidly expanding software market. Customer concentration, regulation, pricing power, capital intensity, and competition can change the meaning of the same financial ratio.

### Company Research Builds the Operating Story

A useful company model needs more than historical revenue and earnings. Analysts break the business into drivers such as unit volume, pricing, users, subscriptions, capacity, utilization, or market share.

Those drivers make the forecast explainable. Instead of assuming revenue grows 12%, the analyst can identify what combination of volume, price, new products, or geographic expansion would produce that result.

### Industry Research Tests Whether the Forecast Is Plausible

Company guidance can be optimistic. Industry research provides an external check.

An analyst covering semiconductor equipment might compare management's outlook with customer capital spending, foundry utilization, memory pricing, and competitor commentary. A bank analyst might track deposit costs, loan growth, credit quality, and interest-rate expectations.

## Financial Statements Turn the Business Story Into Numbers

The SEC identifies four core financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement, and statement of shareholders' equity.

Analysts use them together rather than treating earnings as a complete picture.

### Income Statements Show Profitability

The income statement tracks revenue, expenses, and profit over a period. Analysts use it to study growth, margins, operating leverage, taxes, and earnings per share.

### Balance Sheets Show Financial Position

The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and shareholder equity at a point in time. It helps analysts assess liquidity, leverage, capital intensity, and financial flexibility.

### Cash Flow Statements Test Earnings Quality

Cash flow statements show how cash moves through operating, investing, and financing activities. Strong accounting earnings with weak cash generation can signal working-capital pressure, heavy investment needs, or aggressive accounting assumptions.

## Analysts Convert Historical Data Into Forecasts

Historical results explain what happened. Valuation depends more heavily on what happens next.

Research analysts therefore build forecasts for revenue, margins, earnings, cash flow, and often company-specific operating metrics. The forecast should connect back to observable business drivers rather than rely on arbitrary growth rates.

A simplified forecast can be represented as:

| Step | Example Question |
|---|---|
| Revenue | How fast can sales grow and what drives that growth? |
| Gross Margin | Does mix, pricing, or input cost change profitability? |
| Operating Expenses | Do costs scale slower or faster than revenue? |
| Operating Income | Does growth create operating leverage? |
| Cash Flow | How much accounting profit turns into cash? |
| Capital Spending | How much reinvestment is required? |
| Free Cash Flow | What cash remains after necessary investment? |

The sequence matters because valuation built on an unsupported earnings forecast can look precise while being fundamentally weak.

## Valuation Turns the Forecast Into an Investment Question

After forecasting the business, the analyst needs to estimate what those future results are worth.

Common methods include:

- price-to-earnings comparisons;
- enterprise value to EBITDA;
- enterprise value to sales;
- discounted cash flow;
- dividend discount models;
- price-to-book for some financial companies;
- and sum-of-the-parts valuation for diversified businesses.

No single valuation method works equally well across every industry. High-growth software companies, banks, utilities, commodity producers, and industrial businesses create cash differently and should not automatically be judged on the same metric.

## Analysts Need to Separate Price From Business Quality

A good company can still be a poor investment at the wrong price. A weak company can occasionally become attractive if expectations are sufficiently low and the risk is understood.

That distinction is one reason screening metrics should be used as inputs rather than conclusions. Combining valuation, profitability, growth, and risk through [stock screener metrics](/markets-stocks/stock-screener-metrics-guide/) can help analysts find candidates, but deeper research is still required before a recommendation is defensible.

## What Skills Matter Most for Stock Research Analysts?

CFA Institute identifies data analysis, industry and company analysis, financial analysis, financial modeling, and valuation as core competencies for research analysts.

Those technical skills sit alongside communication. An analyst can build an excellent model and still fail if the conclusion cannot be explained clearly to a portfolio manager, investment committee, or client.

### Accounting and Financial Statement Analysis

Analysts need to understand how reported results are constructed, where management judgment enters the accounts, and which metrics vary across industries.

### Financial Modeling

Models translate assumptions into revenue, earnings, cash flow, and valuation. The goal is not complexity for its own sake. A useful model makes the investment thesis easier to test.

### Industry Knowledge

Coverage improves when analysts understand how customers buy, what drives pricing, which competitors matter, and where regulation or technology can reshape the market.

### Written Communication

Research has to be read. Clear writing helps distinguish the thesis, supporting evidence, catalysts, valuation, and risks without burying the conclusion in detail.

## Qualifications Depend on the Employer and Role

Many analyst roles require a bachelor's degree in finance, accounting, economics, business, mathematics, data science, or a related field. Employers may also value sector-specific experience, coding skills, or advanced credentials.

The CFA Program is particularly relevant because its curriculum covers financial statement analysis, equity investments, valuation, portfolio management, ethics, and quantitative methods.

CFA Institute also notes that candidates who pass CFA Level I and Level II may qualify for an exemption from Part I of FINRA's Series 86 Research Analyst Qualification Exam. The exact licensing requirement depends on the employer and regulatory role.

## What Good Equity Research Looks Like

Strong research is transparent about uncertainty. A report should distinguish historical facts from forecasts and make clear which assumptions drive the conclusion.

A useful structure often includes:

1. the investment thesis;
2. business and industry context;
3. financial forecasts;
4. valuation;
5. catalysts;
6. downside risks;
7. scenario analysis;
8. and the assumptions that would invalidate the thesis.

The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to make uncertainty visible enough that another investor can understand where the conclusion might fail.

## The Analyst's Real Product Is a Decision Framework

Models, reports, earnings estimates, and price targets are outputs. The deeper value of research is the framework connecting evidence to an investment decision.

A strong analyst can explain why revenue should grow, what could pressure margins, which risks matter, how much of the story is already reflected in the price, and what new information would change the view.

That makes stock research less about predicting every quarterly move and more about continually updating probabilities as evidence changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Does a Stock Research Analyst Do?

A stock research analyst studies companies, industries, financial statements, valuation, and market conditions, then builds forecasts and communicates investment conclusions to portfolio managers, clients, or internal decision-makers.

### What Is the Difference Between Buy-Side and Sell-Side Research?

Sell-side analysts generally publish research for external clients, while buy-side analysts work inside investment organizations and use research to support internal portfolio construction, security selection, and position sizing.

### How Much Do Financial and Investment Analysts Earn?

BLS reported median annual pay of $102,740 in May 2025 for financial and investment analysts. Actual equity-research compensation varies substantially by experience, employer, performance, and location.

### Is the CFA Useful for Equity Research?

Yes. The CFA curriculum covers financial analysis, valuation, accounting, ethics, and portfolio management. Employers may value the credential, although requirements vary by role and organization.

### Do Research Analysts Need to Be Good at Math?

Analysts need quantitative comfort, but the role also requires accounting, business judgment, industry knowledge, modeling, and communication. Complex mathematics is less important than using appropriate methods correctly.
