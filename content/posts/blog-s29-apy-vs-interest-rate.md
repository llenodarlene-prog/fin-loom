# APY vs Interest Rate: Why the Numbers Are Different

APY vs interest rate is one of the most common points of confusion in savings. Both numbers describe what an account pays, but they do not measure the same thing.

The **interest rate** is the annual rate applied to the account before compounding is reflected in the return. **APY**, or annual percentage yield, shows the annual yield after including the effect of compounding. That makes APY the more useful number when comparing interest-bearing deposit accounts: if two accounts quote the same interest rate but compound on different schedules, the one that compounds more often can have the higher APY.

Federal Regulation DD makes the distinction explicit. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau defines the interest rate as the annual rate paid on an account **without** reflecting compounding, while APY reflects both the interest rate and the compounding frequency.

## Key Takeaways

- The interest rate shows the stated annual rate before the effect of compounding.
- APY includes compounding and reflects the annual yield more completely.
- With no compounding during the year, APY and the stated rate can be the same.
- At a 4.00% nominal rate, monthly compounding gives about 4.074% APY and daily compounding about 4.081%.
- For comparing savings accounts, money market accounts, and CDs, APY is usually the more useful figure.
- APY does not capture fees, rate conditions, withdrawal limits, or changes to variable rates.
- APR is a separate borrowing-cost measure and should not be confused with APY.

## What Is the Difference Between APY and Interest Rate?

The difference is **compounding**.

Under [Regulation DD](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/2/), an account's interest rate is the annual rate paid without reflecting compounding. APY reflects the interest rate **and** how often interest compounds over the year. That makes APY an annual yield rather than simply a quoted rate.

| Measure | Includes Compounding? | Best Use |
|---|---|---|
| Interest Rate | No | Understanding the stated rate |
| APY | Yes | Comparing annual deposit returns |

If an account compounds only once at the end of the year, the two figures can match. When interest compounds more often, APY can be slightly higher. For a fuller explanation of the yield itself, [APY meaning](/money-banking/apy-meaning/) covers the definition, account types, and disclosure rules.

## A 4% Interest Rate Does Not Always Mean a 4% APY

A worked example makes the APY vs interest rate distinction concrete. Assume three accounts each quote a **4.00% annual interest rate** but compound at different intervals, each holding $10,000 for one year.

| Compounding Frequency | Stated Interest Rate | Approximate APY | Interest on $10,000 After One Year |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Annual | 4.00% | 4.000% | $400.00 |
| Monthly | 4.00% | 4.074% | $407.42 |
| Daily | 4.00% | 4.081% | $408.08 |

These are Fin Loom calculations using the standard compounding formula. The daily-compounding account earns about $8 more than the annually compounded one over a year on $10,000. The difference is modest, but APY makes it visible without anyone having to work out the compounding schedule by hand.

### The Gap Grows With Larger Balances

On $100,000, the difference between 4.00% annual compounding and the roughly 4.081% daily-compounded APY grows from about $8 to about $81 for the year. The rate gap is still small in percentage terms, but the dollar effect scales with the balance, which is why larger savers pay closer attention to it.

### The Gap Grows With Higher Rates

The spread between the stated rate and APY also widens as rates rise. A 5.00% rate compounded monthly gives about 5.116% APY, a gap of 0.116 percentage points, compared with about 0.074 points at 4.00%. Higher rates give compounding more to work with each period.

## Why APY Is Usually the Better Number for Comparing Savings Accounts

APY was designed to help consumers compare deposit products on a consistent basis.

The [CFPB's advertising rules](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/8/) require an advertisement that states a deposit rate of return to state it as an annual percentage yield. An interest rate can also appear, but not more conspicuously than the APY it relates to.

The rule exists because institutions compound interest differently. APY converts those differences into one annual measure. When comparing two high-yield savings accounts with similar features, the higher APY generally points to the stronger annual interest return under the disclosed assumptions.

## How the APY Formula Converts an Interest Rate Into Yield

A common formula for converting a nominal annual rate into APY is:

**APY = (1 + r ÷ n)^n − 1**

Here r is the annual interest rate as a decimal and n is the number of compounding periods per year. For a 4% rate compounded monthly, (1 + 0.04 ÷ 12)^12 − 1 gives about **4.074%**. For the same rate compounded daily, (1 + 0.04 ÷ 365)^365 − 1 gives about **4.081%**.

The [CFPB's Appendix A to Regulation DD](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/a/) sets out the formal APY method institutions use for disclosures. It assumes principal and interest stay on deposit for the term, and it uses a 365-day term for accounts without a stated maturity. To run these numbers with any rate and schedule, the [APY calculator](/money-banking/apy-calculator/) converts an interest rate into APY instantly.

### Going From APY Back to a Rate

The formula also works in reverse. A 5.00% APY on an account that compounds monthly corresponds to a nominal rate of about 4.889%, a Fin Loom calculation. This is why an account can advertise a rate below 5% and an APY of 5% without either figure being wrong.

### Why the Disclosed APY Comes First

A hand calculation is a check, not a substitute. Banks compute disclosed APYs under Regulation DD rules that account for their own crediting practices. When a bank's stated APY and your estimate differ slightly, the disclosed figure is the one to use for comparison.

## When APY and Interest Rate Can Be the Same

APY and interest rate can match when interest is compounded only once during the year. An account paying 4.00% and crediting interest only once a year can show a 4.00% APY.

Once interest is credited during the year and starts earning interest of its own, APY rises above the nominal rate. That is why the gap is usually larger when compounding happens more often.

## Which Number Should You Use When Comparing Accounts?

For savings products, APY is usually the better first comparison because it standardizes the return from interest. That does not mean the highest APY automatically wins.

### Account Terms to Check Next

After comparing APYs, a consumer should also compare these terms:

- **Monthly Fees:** Fees can cancel out a yield advantage.
- **Minimum Balances:** The APY may apply only above a set amount.
- **Direct-Deposit Rules:** Some accounts pay the top rate only with qualifying deposits.
- **Balance Caps:** The highest rate may stop above a threshold.
- **Withdrawal Access:** Limits and CD penalties affect how usable the money is.
- **Fixed or Variable Rate:** A variable APY can change after opening.
- **Deposit Insurance:** Confirm FDIC or NCUA coverage.

These terms often decide which account works better in daily use, even when the APY vs interest rate comparison looks close.

### Matching the Account to the Goal

The right account depends on what the money is for. Cash for emergencies needs access more than the last fraction of a percentage point, while money set aside for a known date may suit a CD. For goals further out, [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) explains when a savings account stops being the right home for the money.

## Why APY Can Change While the Account Stays Open

Most savings-account APYs are variable. A bank can change the underlying interest rate as market conditions and its deposit needs change, and when the rate changes, the APY changes with it.

This differs from many CDs, which generally lock a rate for a set term. A savings account advertised at 4.50% APY today may not keep that yield for a full year, so the APY is a snapshot of current pricing rather than a fixed promise.

## APY vs Interest Rate vs APR

APY and interest rate are deposit-account concepts that often appear together. APR is usually a borrowing-cost measure. The CFPB explains that APR measures a loan's interest rate plus additional fees charged with the loan.

| Measure | Typical Context | Compounding or Fees |
|---|---|---|
| Interest Rate | Savings or borrowing | Quoted rate; does not itself express compounding |
| APY | Savings, CDs, money market accounts | Includes compounding in the annual yield |
| APR | Loans and credit | Includes the interest rate plus certain loan fees |

A useful rule of thumb follows: savers generally look for a **higher APY**, while borrowers generally prefer a **lower APR**, all else equal.

## Why a Higher APY Can Still Produce Lower Actual Earnings

APY assumes conditions that may not match how a person uses the account. Actual earnings can come in lower when money is withdrawn during the year, when the rate falls, when the top APY applies only to part of the balance, when the account charges fees, or when the saver misses a qualification requirement.

### An Illustration With Fees

Suppose one account pays 4.074% APY with a $5 monthly fee and another pays 4.00% APY with no fee. On $10,000 for a year, the first earns about $407.42 in interest but pays $60 in fees, leaving $347.42. The second earns $400.00. The lower APY wins here, an illustrative Fin Loom calculation.

### Treat APY as a Starting Point

For this reason, APY works best as a standard comparison measure rather than a guarantee of exact dollars. Once two accounts are compared on APY, the fees and conditions decide which one leaves more money in the account at the end of the year.

## The Most Useful Number Depends on the Question

The APY vs interest rate choice comes down to the question being asked. If the question is what rate the bank applies before compounding, look at the interest rate. If the question is how much the deposit can earn over a year after compounding, APY is the more useful number.

That distinction is simple, but it prevents a common mistake. An account advertising a higher interest rate is not necessarily offering the better annual yield if another account compounds more often. Comparing APY vs interest rate side by side, and then checking fees and terms, keeps the comparison honest.

## APY vs Interest Rate When the Rate Changes

The APY vs interest rate relationship gets more interesting when an account pays more than one rate. Regulation DD's Appendix A shows how institutions handle this, and its examples make the gap between the two numbers easier to see.

### Stepped Rates on a CD

Appendix A describes a $1,000 six-month CD paying 5% compounded daily for the first 91 days and 5.5% for the next 92 days. Total interest is $26.68, and the disclosed APY is 5.39%. No single quoted interest rate describes that account, yet the APY sums up the whole term in one figure.

### Introductory Rates on Variable Accounts

For a variable-rate account with an introductory rate, Appendix A assumes the introductory rate lasts for its stated period and the regular rate applies for the rest of the year. Its example pays 7% for 91 days and then 5%, giving $56.52 of interest on $1,000 and an APY of 5.65%, well below the 7% headline rate.

## Tiered Rates and the APY vs Interest Rate Gap

Some accounts pay different interest rates on different balance levels. Appendix A explains two ways institutions apply tiers, and each one changes what the APY means for a given balance.

### Paying One Rate on the Full Balance

Under the first method in Appendix A, the account pays the tier's rate on the entire balance. With rates of 5.25%, 5.50%, and 5.75% across three tiers, an $8,000 deposit earns 5.50% on all of it, and the disclosed APY for that tier is 5.65%. Moving into a higher tier lifts the yield on every dollar.

### Paying Each Rate Only Within Its Tier

Under the second method, each rate applies only to the part of the balance inside its tier. The institution must then disclose a range of APYs for each tier, such as 5.39% to 5.61% for the middle tier in the CFPB's example. Here the APY vs interest rate comparison depends on the exact balance.

## Reading the Yield on a Statement

The APY vs interest rate comparison also shows up on account statements, where a different yield figure appears.

### APY Earned on a Statement

Appendix A also defines the "annual percentage yield earned" shown on periodic statements. It reflects the interest credited during the statement period and the average daily balance. In the CFPB's example, an account holding $1,500 for 15 days and $500 for 15 days averages $1,000, earns $5.25 in a 30-day period, and shows an APY earned of 6.58%.

### Why Statement Yields Can Differ

The disclosed APY assumes the balance stays put, while the APY earned reflects what really happened in the period. If deposits or withdrawals move the balance, the two figures can differ. Comparing them shows whether the account delivered its advertised yield on the money you held.

## How Interest Payouts Affect APY

How interest is paid can also change the APY vs interest rate gap.

### When Interest Must Be Withdrawn

Appendix A normally assumes principal and interest stay on deposit for the term. That assumption is dropped if an account requires interest to be withdrawn, in which case the APY reflects the withdrawal requirement. Interest paid out instead of compounded earns nothing further, so the yield is lower than for the same rate left to compound.

### Long CDs That Pay Out Interest

For time accounts longer than a year that do not compound and must pay interest out at least annually, Appendix A lets the APY be disclosed as equal to the interest rate. Its example is a $1,000 two-year CD paying 6% by check every six months, which may show a 6.00% APY.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is APY Higher Than the Interest Rate?

APY is often slightly higher because it includes compounding. If interest compounds only once a year, the APY and the stated annual interest rate can be identical.

### Should I Compare APY or Interest Rate for Savings?

Compare APY first, because it includes compounding. Then review fees, minimum balances, rate conditions, withdrawal access, and whether the rate is fixed or variable.

### Why Is My APY Different From My Interest Rate?

The difference comes from compounding. APY counts interest earned on interest already credited during the year, while the stated interest rate does not include that effect.

### Can APY Change During the Year?

Yes. Variable-rate savings and money market accounts can change their APY whenever the underlying rate changes. Fixed-rate CDs generally keep the agreed rate until maturity.

### Is APY the Same as APR?

No. APY measures deposit yield including compounding, while APR measures borrowing cost and can include a loan's interest rate plus certain fees charged by the lender.
