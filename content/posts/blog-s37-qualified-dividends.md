# Qualified Dividends: 2026 Tax Rates, Holding Rules, and Examples

Qualified dividends are ordinary dividends that meet specific IRS tests and are taxed at the same 0%, 15%, or 20% federal rates that apply to net capital gain. For 2026, which rate applies depends on taxable income, filing status, and how the dividends stack on top of other income.

A dividend does not qualify just because a U.S. public company paid it. The payer has to be an eligible corporation, the payment must not fall into an excluded category, and the shareholder generally has to hold the stock for more than 60 days in a set window around the ex-dividend date.

This page explains U.S. federal rules for general education. It is not tax advice, and state taxes and personal circumstances can change the result.

## Key Takeaways

- Qualified dividends are a subset of ordinary dividends, not a separate kind of income.
- They are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%, the same maximum rates that apply to net capital gain.
- For 2026, a single filer's 0% band runs to $49,450 of taxable income, and the 15% band runs to $545,500.
- Common stock generally must be held more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date.
- Some preferred stock uses a longer test: more than 90 days during a 181-day period.
- Form 1099-DIV shows qualified dividends in box 1b, and that amount is already included in box 1a.
- Credit union "dividends" are reported as interest, and only part of a REIT or fund distribution may qualify.
- Higher-income taxpayers can also owe the separate 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax.

## What Is a Qualified Dividend?

A qualified dividend is an ordinary dividend that receives capital gain tax rates instead of ordinary income tax rates. IRS [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) defines qualified dividends as "the ordinary dividends that are subject to the same 0%, 15%, or 20% maximum tax rate that applies to net capital gain."

Publication 550 sets three conditions. All three must be met:

- **Eligible Payer:** The dividend was paid by a U.S. corporation or a qualified foreign corporation.
- **Not Excluded:** The dividend is not one of the types the IRS lists as never qualifying.
- **Holding Period:** The shareholder meets the required holding period for the stock.

When all three tests pass, the dividend is taxed at the lower rates. When any test fails, the same cash payment is taxed as ordinary income. The amount of the check does not change; only the tax rate does.

## Qualified Dividend Tax Rates for 2026

The 2026 thresholds come from Revenue Procedure 2025-32, published in [Internal Revenue Bulletin 2025-45](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-45_IRB). The capital gain brackets set there are the same bands used for qualified dividends.

The table shows the top of the 0% band and the top of the 15% band. Amounts above the 15% ceiling fall in the 20% band.

| Filing Status | 0% Rate Up To | 15% Rate Up To | 20% Rate Above |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Single | $49,450 | $545,500 | $545,500 |
| Married Filing Jointly and Surviving Spouse | $98,900 | $613,700 | $613,700 |
| Married Filing Separately | $49,450 | $306,850 | $306,850 |
| Head of Household | $66,200 | $579,600 | $579,600 |

These are taxable-income figures, not gross-income figures. Taxable income is what remains after deductions, so a household can earn more than these amounts in wages and still have some qualified dividends land in the 0% band.

## How Qualified Dividends Stack on Other Income

The rate on qualified dividends is not chosen by salary alone. The IRS worksheet places ordinary income first and then stacks qualified dividends and net capital gain on top. Each slice of the stacked amount takes the rate of the band it falls into.

### A Worked 2026 Example

Take a single filer with $40,000 of taxable ordinary income and $20,000 of qualified dividends, for $60,000 of total taxable income. In this Fin Loom illustrative calculation, the first $9,450 of dividends fills the 0% band up to $49,450. The remaining $10,550 falls in the 15% band, for $1,582.50 of tax on the dividends.

### What the Total Tax Looks Like

Using the 2026 single brackets from Revenue Procedure 2025-32, tax on the $40,000 of ordinary income is about $4,552. Adding $1,582.50 gives roughly $6,134.50 of federal income tax. The IRS tax table rounds amounts, so a filed return can differ slightly. Tax software or the IRS worksheet makes the official calculation.

## Qualified Dividends Are Still Ordinary Dividends

The vocabulary trips up many readers. On a tax form, "ordinary dividends" is the total of all taxable dividends, and qualified dividends sit inside that total. The word "ordinary" here describes the reporting category, not the tax rate.

The [Form 1099-DIV instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099div) say box 1a "includes amounts entered in boxes 1b," and box 1b holds "the portion of the dividends in box 1a that qualifies for the reduced capital gains rates." Two boxes on the form show how this works:

- **Box 1a:** Total ordinary dividends, including any qualified portion.
- **Box 1b:** The part of box 1a the payer reports as qualified.

A common mistake is adding box 1b to box 1a. That counts the same dollars twice. Box 1b is a subset of box 1a and is only used to apply the lower rate.

## Where Qualified Dividends Go on Form 1040

The Form 1040 instructions tell filers to enter total qualified dividends on line 3a. Qualified dividends are also included in the ordinary dividend total on line 3b. The lower rate is then applied through the Qualified Dividends and Capital Gain Tax Worksheet or Schedule D.

Publication 550 adds that Schedule B is required when ordinary dividends in box 1a are more than $1,500. Line numbers and thresholds can change, so filers should use the instructions for the tax year being filed.

## The 61-Day Holding Period Rule

For most common stock, Publication 550 says "you must have held the stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date." The 1099-DIV instructions describe the same test as holding the stock for at least 61 days in that window.

The counting rule is specific. Publication 550 says to "include the day you disposed of the stock, but not the day you acquired it." The purchase day does not count, but the sale day does.

### Why the Window Surrounds the Ex-Dividend Date

The ex-dividend date is the first day a buyer no longer receives the upcoming dividend. The 121-day window starts 60 days before that date, so the test looks at ownership both before and after it. The rule stops a trader from buying just before the ex-dividend date, collecting the payment, and selling days later at the lower rate.

### Receiving the Dividend Is a Separate Test

A shareholder can be entitled to the cash dividend and still fail the qualified test. Someone who buys shortly before the ex-dividend date and sells three weeks later keeps the payment, but it is taxed as ordinary income. Entitlement depends on the record date, while the tax rate depends on the holding period.

## Preferred Stock Can Have a 91-Day Rule

Some preferred stock uses a longer test. For preferred dividends due to periods totaling more than 366 days, Publication 550 requires holding the stock "more than 90 days during the 181-day period that begins 90 days before the ex-dividend date."

If the preferred dividends are due to periods totaling less than 367 days, the standard common-stock test applies instead. Preferred share terms vary widely, so the offering documents and the payer's tax reporting are the place to confirm which test applies.

## Days With Reduced Risk Do Not Count

The holding period does not simply count calendar days of ownership. Publication 550 says certain days are excluded when the shareholder's risk of loss is reduced. Examples it lists include:

- **Put Options:** Holding an option to sell the same stock.
- **Short Sales:** Being in a short sale of substantially identical stock.
- **Offsetting Positions:** Holding positions in substantially similar or related property that reduce the risk of loss.

These rules mainly affect hedged positions. A shareholder who simply owns the stock outright counts ordinary calendar days. Anyone using options or other hedges around a dividend may need a tax professional to apply the exclusions.

## Which Companies Can Pay Qualified Dividends?

The payer test is the first of the three conditions. It covers two groups of corporations, and each group carries its own limits.

### U.S. Corporations

Dividends from U.S. corporations generally meet the payer test. That covers most large public companies that pay regular cash dividends. The shareholder still has to meet the holding period, and the payment still must not be an excluded type, so a U.S. payer alone does not make a dividend qualify.

### Qualified Foreign Corporations

Publication 550 says a foreign corporation can be qualified if it is incorporated in a U.S. territory, is eligible for benefits under a comprehensive U.S. income tax treaty that includes an exchange of information program, or has stock readily tradable on an established U.S. securities market. A passive foreign investment company in the year of payment or the prior year does not qualify.

## ADR Dividends Can Sometimes Qualify

An American depositary receipt, or ADR, represents shares of a foreign company and trades in the United States. Publication 550 says stock or an ADR is readily tradable when it is listed on a national securities exchange registered under section 6 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, or on the Nasdaq Stock Market.

Meeting the tradability condition is only one test. The foreign company must still not be a passive foreign investment company, and the shareholder still has to meet the holding period. The broker's 1099-DIV shows whether the payer reported the ADR dividend as qualified.

## Which Dividends Are Not Qualified?

Publication 550 lists payments that are never qualified dividends. It also warns that these payments "are not qualified dividends even if they are shown on Form 1099-DIV, box 1b." The list includes:

- **Capital Gain Distributions:** These are taxed under capital gain rules, not as qualified dividends.
- **Deposit Dividends:** Amounts paid on deposits at mutual savings banks, cooperative banks, credit unions, and similar institutions.
- **Exempt Organizations:** Dividends from a corporation that is a tax-exempt organization or farmer's cooperative in the year paid or the prior year.
- **ESOP Dividends:** Dividends a corporation receives a deduction for because they are paid on employer securities in an employee stock ownership plan.
- **Related Payments:** Dividends on stock where the shareholder is obligated to make related payments, such as under a short sale.
- **Payments in Lieu:** Payments received instead of dividends, which can happen when shares are lent out.

Dividends that fail the holding period also lose qualified treatment. A payment called a "dividend" does not receive the lower rate just because of its name.

## Credit Union Dividends Are Usually Interest

Credit unions often call the earnings on savings accounts "dividends." For federal income tax, Publication 550 says to report these amounts as interest income, and they generally appear on Form 1099-INT. They are taxed at ordinary income rates.

This is a useful example of why labels can mislead. The financial institution's wording does not decide the tax treatment. The tax category comes from the type of payment and the rules that apply to it.

## REIT and Fund Distributions Need a Closer Look

Real estate investment trusts and mutual funds pass income through to shareholders, and their distributions can mix several tax components. Only part of a REIT or fund distribution may qualify, and the qualified share is reported in box 1b of Form 1099-DIV.

### REIT Distributions

Many REIT distributions are not qualified dividends. A REIT typically issues year-end tax information that splits its distributions into components. That is why a 6% REIT yield and a 6% qualified corporate dividend can leave different amounts after federal tax, even though the headline percentages match.

### Mutual Fund and ETF Distributions

A mutual fund or ETF can pass through qualified dividends it received from the companies it owns. The fund reports the qualified portion in box 1b. The shareholder must also meet the holding period for the fund shares, so frequent trading in fund shares can reduce the qualified amount even when the fund reports it.

## Reinvested Dividends Are Still Taxable

Reinvesting does not delay the tax. Publication 550 says that if dividends are used to buy more stock at fair market value, "you must still report the dividends as income." A $500 qualified dividend that buys new shares through a reinvestment plan is still $500 of dividend income for that year.

Each reinvested purchase also creates a new tax lot with its own basis and holding period. ## Qualified Dividends Can Still Face the NIIT

The 0%, 15%, and 20% rates are not always the full federal bill. The IRS describes the Net Investment Income Tax as a 3.8% tax on the lesser of net investment income or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds a threshold. Dividends are included in net investment income.

The IRS lists these thresholds:

| Filing Status | NIIT Threshold |
|---|---:|
| Single or Head of Household | $200,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $250,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $125,000 |
| Qualifying Surviving Spouse | $250,000 |

Because the NIIT is calculated separately, a qualified dividend taxed at 20% can carry an additional 3.8% for a taxpayer above these limits.

## Qualified vs Ordinary Dividends Compared

The difference comes down to which tests a dividend meets and how it is taxed. The table compares the two on the points that appear on a tax return.

| Feature | Qualified Dividend | Nonqualified Ordinary Dividend |
|---|---|---|
| Federal Rate | 0%, 15%, or 20% | Ordinary income tax rates |
| Holding Period Test | Required | Not required for the ordinary rate |
| Payer Test | U.S. or qualified foreign corporation | Any payer |
| Form 1099-DIV | Boxes 1a and 1b | Box 1a only |
| NIIT Exposure | Possible | Possible |
| State Tax | Depends on the state | Depends on the state |

The federal tests in this table do not decide state tax. Each state sets its own treatment of dividend income, so the state row depends on where the taxpayer files.

## Do Qualified Dividends Count as Income?

Yes. Qualified dividends are taxable income and are reported on the return in full. "Qualified" means the dividend is eligible for lower federal rates when all the tests are met. It does not mean the dividend is excluded from income.

Qualified dividends also count toward adjusted gross income and modified adjusted gross income. That can affect other parts of a return, such as the NIIT threshold, even when the dividends themselves fall in the 0% band.

## Why the Tax Year Must Be Named

The thresholds change each year through inflation adjustments. The 2026 figures on this page come from Revenue Procedure 2025-32, and using an earlier year's thresholds can produce the wrong estimate.

For 2026, the 0% ceiling is $49,450 for single filers, $98,900 for joint filers, and $66,200 for heads of household. Any estimate of tax on qualified dividends should state which tax year it uses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Are Qualified Dividends?

Qualified dividends are ordinary dividends that meet IRS payer, exclusion, and holding period tests, so they are taxed at the 0%, 15%, or 20% capital gain rates.

### What Is the Qualified Dividend Tax Rate in 2026?

It is 0%, 15%, or 20%. A single filer pays 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income and 15% up to $545,500, with 20% above that.

### How Long Must I Hold a Stock for a Qualified Dividend?

For common stock, more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. Count the sale day but not the purchase day.

### Are REIT Dividends Qualified?

Many REIT distributions are not qualified. Only part of a REIT or fund distribution may qualify, and the qualified portion appears in box 1b of Form 1099-DIV.

### Where Are Qualified Dividends Shown on Form 1099-DIV?

They appear in box 1b. That amount is already included in the total ordinary dividends in box 1a, so the two boxes should never be added together.
