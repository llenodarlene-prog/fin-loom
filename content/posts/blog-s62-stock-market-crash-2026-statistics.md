# Stock Market Crash 2026: 12 Statistics From the October and November Selloff

A 5.5% one-day drop in the Nasdaq Composite on Thursday, November 5, 2026 made the selloff look like a market-wide collapse. The month before told a more mixed story. During October, some of the largest technology shares fell hard while most other parts of the U.S. market rose.

That difference shapes any honest answer to a stock market crash 2026 search, and it is why this page treats each number separately. A sharp one-day fall, a correction from a recent high and a negative monthly return measure different things. Mixing them makes the damage look either bigger or smaller than it was. This page sets out 12 dated figures, says exactly which window each one covers, and explains what they do and do not show. All data runs through the close on Friday, November 6, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **October was a rotation, not a collapse:** the S&P 500 fell 0.9% in October 2026, while the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 5.0%.
- **Technology led the losses:** the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3% in October and the semiconductor index fell 15.3%.
- **November 5 was the sharpest day:** the S&P 500 fell 4.4% and the Nasdaq 5.5% in a single session.
- **The Nasdaq entered a correction:** its November 5 close was 11.9% below its October 6 record close, by our calculation from FRED data.
- **Not a bear market:** none of the major indexes had fallen 20% from its high as of November 6, 2026.

## What Happened in the October and November 2026 Decline?

October brought a rotation away from concentrated technology leadership. November 5 then delivered a broad, severe one-day selloff led by AI-related stocks. The Nasdaq's close that day was more than 10% below its October record, which meets the common threshold for a correction.

That does not mean every index suffered a 20% bear market, or that November's monthly result was known. As of the November 6 close, November was only four trading sessions old. Any figure for "November 2026" before month-end is a partial-month reading and should be labeled that way.

### The Two Phases in Plain Terms

The first phase, across October, was mostly a change in leadership. Money moved out of large technology and communication companies and into energy, consumer staples, utilities and health care. The headline S&P 500 dipped slightly while the average stock rose.

The second phase, on November 5, was broader. Large technology stocks fell again, but so did the Dow and smaller companies. A concentrated selloff turned into a wide one in a single session, which is why that day drew so much attention.

### Why the Dates Change the Answer

October figures cover September 30 to October 30, 2026, the last trading day of the month. The November 5 figures cover one session, from the November 4 close to the November 5 close. The correction figure compares the November 5 close with the October 6 record close. Three windows, three different questions.

FRED's daily closes let readers check the monthly numbers. The S&P 500 closed at 7,651.54 on September 30 and 7,580.07 on October 30, a 0.93% price decline. The Nasdaq Composite went from 26,861.06 to 25,437.00, a 5.30% decline. These are price changes without dividends, so a total-return figure can differ by a few hundredths of a point.

### What the Dow Figure Shows

The Dow's October gain stands out against the Nasdaq's loss. FRED's price series shows a 1.70% rise from September 30 to October 30, close to the 1.8% figure in Nasdaq's review, which may include dividends or use a slightly different endpoint. Either way, the Dow, a price-weighted index of 30 companies, rose while the Nasdaq fell.

## Why Did an Index Fall When Most Sectors Rose?

The S&P 500 weights its members by market value. A drop in a very large company can outweigh gains spread across many smaller ones. The equal-weight version gives each member roughly the same influence at each rebalance.

In October, that design difference produced a 5.9 percentage point gap between the equal-weight S&P 500 (+5.0%) and the standard S&P 500 (-0.9%). Janus Henderson noted it was the equal-weight index's biggest monthly lead over the standard index since April 2009. Communication Services and Information Technology were the only two of eleven sectors to fall, so nine rose.

### Two Investors, Two Different Octobers

An investor holding mainly large technology names would have felt October as a sharp loss. An investor holding a broad mix of value and defensive shares might have had a positive month. The headline index cannot tell either person what happened in their own account, which is why a single "market return" can mislead.

## What Changed on November 5?

The selloff widened. FRED's daily closes show the Dow fell 3.44% that Thursday, from 53,356.12 to 51,523.06, alongside the S&P 500 and Nasdaq declines. The Associated Press reported a 4.4% fall in the Russell 2000 the same day. Losses that had been concentrated in technology spread to industrial and smaller companies.

Reuters described the move as a repricing of AI infrastructure expectations, linked to political and financing uncertainty after the U.S. midterm elections. Those reports describe what investors were weighing. Price data alone cannot prove a single cause, and any explanation of one day's move should be read as a reported factor, not a settled fact.

### One Session Versus One Month

October's S&P 500 loss was 0.9% across a whole month. The index then fell about 4.4% in one November session. The second number does not replace the first. It covers a different window, and adding the two together does not produce a meaningful "November return."

### The Next Day

The Nasdaq Composite closed at 24,579.42 on Friday, November 6, about 1.1% above its November 5 close, according to FRED. The Dow slipped to 51,285.73. A partial rebound in one index and a further dip in another is a reminder that the day after a sharp fall rarely tells a clean story.

## Does the Word Crash Fit 2026?

"Crash" is a description, not an official statistic. A correction is commonly defined as a fall of at least 10% from a recent high. A bear market is commonly defined as a fall of at least 20%. Neither threshold is set in law; they are conventions used by analysts and the press.

By our calculation from FRED's daily closes, the Nasdaq Composite's November 5 close of 24,315.00 was 11.9% below its October 6 record close of 27,599.89. That meets the correction threshold. The S&P 500's November 5 close was about 6.0% below its own October 6 high, short of a correction. The Dow was about 2.5% below its October 22 high.

### What a Correction Does and Does Not Tell You

A correction confirms that prices have fallen a set amount from a peak. It does not predict whether they will fall further, recover quickly or enter a bear market. Past corrections have ended in all three ways, so the label is a description of the past, not a forecast.

## How to Read Market Headlines During a Selloff

The stock market crash 2026 headlines that spread on November 5 often blended several measures into one alarming line. A headline number is a starting point. Before reacting, check four things: which index it refers to, what window it covers, whether dividends are included, and whether it measures a single day or a drop from a peak.

### Questions to Ask About Any Market Figure

Is the figure from the previous close, the start of the year or an earlier high? Is it a price return or a total return? Does it describe a market-value-weighted index dominated by a few companies, or a broader measure? Brokerage apps often default to the previous close, while news reports may use a peak.

### Looking Inside the Index

When losses are concentrated in a few heavily weighted companies, the headline can hide strength elsewhere. When losses widen across company sizes and sectors, as they did on November 5, risk looks different. Comparing the standard S&P 500 with its equal-weight version is a quick way to see which situation applies.

## What the Stock Market Crash 2026 Headlines Mean for a Long-Term Plan

The stock market crash 2026 coverage focused on how far prices fell, but a long-term plan depends on more than one week. A sharp drop can tempt investors to act quickly. A written plan helps separate a deliberate decision from a reaction to one day's headline. The questions below are general, not personal advice.

First, check whether any money you need within the next few years is invested in stocks. Money needed soon is usually better held in cash or other stable assets, because a correction can last longer than expected. Second, check how concentrated your portfolio is. If a few large technology companies make up much of your holdings, a selloff led by those companies will hit harder.

Third, review whether your mix still matches your target. FinLoom's guide to [stock analysis fundamentals](/markets-stocks/stock-analysis-fundamentals/) explains how to look at the businesses behind a fund or index before deciding anything. A fall in stocks shifts the balance between stocks, bonds and cash. Some investors rebalance on a set schedule rather than in response to news. Whatever approach you use, it works best when decided before a selloff, not during one. Writing the rule down, such as rebalancing when any holding drifts five points from its target, makes it easier to follow when headlines are loud. That five-point figure is only an example, not a recommended threshold.

## Common Mistakes When Reading a Stock Market Crash 2026 Headline

The first mistake is adding returns from different windows. October's monthly figure and November 5's daily figure cannot be summed into a meaningful total. The second is treating one index as "the market." In October, the Nasdaq fell 5.3% while the Dow and equal-weight S&P 500 rose.

The third is reading a correction as a certain path to a bear market. The fourth is assuming the reported cause is proven. News reports describe the factors investors discussed, but price moves usually reflect several forces at once. Keeping these distinctions in mind makes any stock market crash 2026 figure easier to interpret.

Year-to-date context does not soften the sharp fall on November 5, but it shows that a stock market crash 2026 headline described a pullback within a year that had been positive for most major U.S. indexes until then. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Was October 2026 a Stock Market Crash?

Not across the whole market. The Nasdaq fell 5.3% in October, but the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 5.0% and nine of eleven sectors gained, according to Janus Henderson.

### How Much Did the Nasdaq Fall on November 5, 2026?

The Nasdaq Composite fell about 5.5% in that single session, closing at 24,315.00 according to FRED. That is a one-day change, not a monthly figure.

### Did the Nasdaq Enter a Correction?

Yes. Its November 5 close was 11.9% below its October 6 record close, by our calculation from FRED data. A correction is commonly defined as a 10% fall.

### Did the S&P 500 Enter a Bear Market?

No. As of November 6, 2026, the S&P 500 was about 6% below its October high, well short of the 20% decline commonly used to define a bear market.

### Is the Stock Market Crash 2026 Over?

Nobody can know in advance. A correction describes a past decline, not the next move. Prices can recover, stay flat or fall further after any selloff.
