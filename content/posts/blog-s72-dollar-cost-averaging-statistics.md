# Dollar Cost Averaging Statistics: Does Investing Regularly Beat Waiting?

A regular contribution can make investing easier to keep up. It cannot make a market loss disappear. That gap is the heart of the debate between dollar cost averaging and investing a lump sum all at once.

[FINRA describes dollar cost averaging](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/dollar-cost-averaging) as investing equal amounts at regular intervals regardless of what the market is doing. It is mainly a contribution rule. The common claim that it always earns better returns needs real qualification, and the best research on the question points the other way for money you already have. This page sets out what the evidence says, where it stops, and how to read it for your own situation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Lump sum usually won in Vanguard's tests:** investing a lump sum at once beat a three-month cost-averaging split about two-thirds of the time across 1976 to 2022.
- **Cash has a cost:** U.S. stocks beat 3-month Treasury bills 76% of the time and bonds beat them 68% of the time over the same period, in Vanguard's figures.
- **Averaging still beat sitting in cash:** the cost-averaging approach outperformed holding cash 69% of the time in Vanguard's global comparison.
- **Payday investing is a different decision:** money that has not been earned yet cannot be invested up front, so regular contributions from wages are not a timing bet.
- **A lower average cost is not a higher return:** fixed contributions buy more shares when prices fall, but the account can still lose value.

## What Is Dollar Cost Averaging?

Dollar cost averaging means putting the same dollar amount into an investment on a fixed schedule, such as $500 on the first of every month. Because the amount stays fixed while the price moves, each contribution buys more shares when the price is low and fewer when it is high.

The term covers two quite different situations. One is a worker investing part of every paycheck. The other is an investor who already holds a large sum and chooses to feed it into the market in slices. Most of the debate, and most of the research, concerns the second case.

### Regular Contributions From Income

A 401(k) deferral or an automatic monthly transfer to a brokerage account is the most common form of the habit. The money arrives in stages because income arrives in stages. There is no lump sum to compare against, so the question of whether to wait never really comes up for this investor.

### Spreading Out a Lump Sum

An inheritance, a bonus or the proceeds of a home sale create a real choice. The investor can put all of it to work today or hold some in cash and invest it over several weeks or months. This is the decision Vanguard studied, and it is where the statistics below apply most directly.

## What Do Dollar Cost Averaging Statistics Show?

One widely cited study is [Vanguard's February 2023 paper](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf) by Megan Finlay and Josef Zorn, which compared investing a lump sum immediately with spreading it across three months. The table summarizes its main figures and the population each one describes.

| Finding | Figure | What it describes |
|---|---|---|
| Lump sum beat cost averaging | About two-thirds of the time | Historical and simulated tests, 1976 to 2022 |
| Lump sum beat cost averaging, global index | 68% | Wealth after one year, rolling periods |
| Cost averaging beat staying in cash | 69% | Same global comparison |
| U.S. stocks beat 3-month Treasury bills | 76% | 1976 to 2022 |
| U.S. bonds beat 3-month Treasury bills | 68% | 1976 to 2022 |

Source: Vanguard, "Cost averaging: Invest now or temporarily hold your cash?" (2023). Outperformance compares wealth after one year.

The study covered the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada and the European Union, with similar results in each. Its headline is narrow but strong: when the cash is already available, putting it to work sooner has usually come out ahead.

### How Large Was the Gap?

Vanguard also measured the size of the advantage. In a 60% stock and 40% bond portfolio starting with $100,000, a three-month cost-averaging plan ended the year at an average of $107,453. The lump sum averaged $109,360, which is 1.8% more. At the median, the gap was 2.2% for an all-stock portfolio and 1.2% for a 40% stock portfolio.

### When Cost Averaging Came Out Ahead

The lump sum did not always win. In the worst historical outcomes, below roughly the 25th percentile, spreading the money out left the investor with more wealth after a year. Those are the periods when the market fell soon after the start date, so the later slices bought at lower prices.

## Why Does Investing Available Money Sooner Often Win?

Stocks and bonds have historically earned more than cash over most periods, though returns swing widely from year to year. [Aswath Damodaran's NYU Stern data](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html) show the S&P 500 compounded at about 10.0% a year from 1928 to 2025, including dividends. A lump sum starts earning that extra return at once. A staggered plan keeps part of the money in cash while it waits for the next purchase date.

Vanguard calls this the opportunity cost of lost risk premium. The longer the waiting period, the more of that premium the investor gives up, so spreading money over twelve months has historically cost more than spreading it over three.

### The Role of Cash Interest

Interest on the waiting cash narrows the gap but did not close it in Vanguard's tests. Using the 3-month Treasury bill rate as the cash return, a lump sum still beat the three-month plan 65% of the time for an all-equity portfolio. As the assumed cash rate rose, the lump sum advantage shrank, all else equal.

### Why the Holding Period Has Little Effect

Once the cost-averaging period ends, both portfolios hold the same mix of assets. Whichever has more money at that point stays ahead in percentage terms from then on, assuming no other changes. A longer holding period can widen or narrow the dollar gap, but it does not reverse the order.

## A Labeled Example of Fixed Contributions

The example below is hypothetical. It shows the mechanism, not a market result. An investor puts $200 into an investment on three dates. Fractional shares are allowed, and fees and taxes are left out.

| Purchase | Hypothetical price | Contribution | Shares bought |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| First | $20 | $200 | 10.000 |
| Second | $16 | $200 | 12.500 |
| Third | $25 | $200 | 8.000 |
| **Total** | Varies | **$600** | **30.500** |

The average cost per share is about $19.67, which is $600 divided by 30.5 shares. The simple average of the three prices is $20.33. The investor paid less per share than the average price because the fixed $200 bought more shares at $16.

### What the Example Shows

Fixed amounts automatically tilt purchases toward lower prices. That is a real arithmetic feature of dollar cost averaging, and it explains why the method feels reassuring in a falling market. It does not mean the investor made money, only that the average cost sits below the average quoted price.

### What the Example Does Not Show

If the price later falls to $10, the 30.5 shares are worth $305, a loss of almost half. And if the price had jumped to $25 before the second purchase and stayed there, putting all $600 in on day one would have bought 30 shares at $20 and ended ahead. The method changes the entry path, not the investment risk.

## Where Did the Money Come From?

Most articles on this topic skip one question. Imagine two people. One receives a $12,000 inheritance today. The other can set aside $1,000 from each of the next twelve paychecks. Both may end up investing $12,000, but only the first person can choose between today and later.

A study that treats the second person as if $12,000 were available on day one creates a misleading comparison. For that person, automatic monthly investing is a budgeting habit, not a market-timing decision, and the Vanguard results do not argue against it.

### Cash Drag Applies to Idle Money

Cash drag is the return lost on money deliberately held out of a portfolio. Future paychecks are not idle portfolio cash because they do not exist yet. The concept only applies when someone has the money and chooses not to invest it.

### Workplace Plans Work This Way

Payroll deductions into a retirement plan are the clearest real-world case. Each deferral is invested as it arrives, so most retirement saving already follows a regular schedule by design. The worker never holds the full year's contributions in cash waiting for a better price.

## Can Dollar Cost Averaging Reduce Risk?

It can reduce the risk of putting an entire lump sum into the market just before a large fall. Vanguard notes that cost averaging temporarily lowers a portfolio's risk by holding some cash while the money is phased in. It does not remove market risk once the money is invested.

The holdings still decide how much the portfolio can lose. A cost-averaged position in a single stock can fall just as far as a lump sum in that stock once the last slice is bought.

### The Behavioral Case

Vanguard's paper argues that for loss-averse investors, lowering risk early can limit regret in a severe downturn and help them stay with the plan. A schedule set in advance can also curb the urge to stop contributing after scary headlines or to pile in only after a rally. That discipline has real value even when the return math favors a lump sum.

### The Trade-Off in Plain Terms

Vanguard frames the choice as protection from regret weighed against an expected lower return. Neither side is free. An investor who would sell in panic after a sudden 20% drop may be better served by a slower start that they can stick with, while one who can hold through swings has usually earned more by starting at once.

## What Should a Fair Backtest Include?

Many online calculators and charts compare strategies without stating their rules. A fair test should use total returns, including dividends, rather than price changes alone. It should also test many start dates rather than one that happens to sit just before a crash.

At minimum, a reader should be able to see whether the test invests an existing lump sum or money that arrives over time, whether idle cash earns interest, the asset mix, fees, dividend reinvestment and the final valuation date.

### Watch for a Hand-Picked Start

Starting a test at the 2007 market peak makes cost averaging look brilliant. Starting it at the March 2009 low makes it look costly. Rolling periods, which test every possible start date, give a much fairer picture, and that is the method Vanguard used.

### Nominal Versus Real Results

A test should say whether results are in today's dollars or the dollars of each period. Inflation changes the meaning of an ending balance over long horizons, and mixing the two can make one strategy look better than it was. ## How Is This Different From Buying the Dip?

Dollar cost averaging follows the calendar. Buying the dip follows the price. One invests on set dates whatever the market does; the other waits for a fall and then acts. FinLoom's [buy the dip statistics](/investing/buy-the-dip-statistics/) explain why a decline is only clear in hindsight.

A dip strategy can leave money in cash for long stretches when no qualifying fall arrives. That is the same cash drag that hurts a staggered lump sum, often for longer, because the trigger may never come.

### Combining the Two

Some investors keep a regular contribution and add extra money only after large falls. That can work as a written rule, but the extra money still sits in cash while it waits. The cost of waiting should be counted, not just the price paid when the trigger finally hits.

### Rebalancing as a Third Option

Rebalancing back to a target mix buys more of whatever has fallen without any forecast. It uses money already in the portfolio rather than fresh cash, and it follows a plan set in advance. For many long-term investors it does the job dip buying promises, with fewer judgment calls.

## Costs That Can Change the Answer

Splitting one purchase into many can add costs. Most large U.S. brokers now charge no commission on stock and ETF trades, so the dollar fee per trade is often zero. Fund costs still apply every year, and a high expense ratio eats into either approach equally.

FinLoom's [expense ratio statistics](/investing/expense-ratio-statistics/) show how small annual percentages compound over decades. The fund chosen usually has more effect on the outcome than whether money goes in over three months or on one day.

### Taxes on Purchases

Buying in a taxable account creates separate tax lots at each price. That can help later, because an investor can choose which lots to sell. It also means more records to keep, so any plan should include a way to track cost basis.

### Minimum Purchase Rules

Some mutual funds set a minimum for the first purchase or for later additions. A plan that splits money into small slices may run into those limits. Checking the fund's rules before choosing a schedule avoids surprises, and an ETF bought through a broker that allows fractional shares usually has no such floor.

## When Might Regular Investing Fit Better?

Automatic contributions suit workers building savings from wages, people who want a repeatable process and anyone who might otherwise leave long-term money idle waiting for certainty. A lump sum may suit an investor who already holds the money and can tolerate swings without selling.

Other needs come first in either case. An emergency reserve, expensive debt and near-term spending usually outweigh the small average gap between entry schedules. The purpose of the money should drive the decision, not the latest headline.

### Questions to Ask First

When will this money be needed? How would a 20% drop in the first month feel? Is there high-interest debt that should be paid first? Is the money already in hand, or will it arrive over time? Honest answers often settle the choice before any statistic does.

### A Middle Path

Vanguard's data showed that longer phase-in periods cost more. An investor who wants some protection from regret can use a short, fixed schedule, such as three months, rather than an open-ended wait. Writing the dates down in advance keeps the plan from turning into market timing.

## How the Evidence Fits Long-Term Investing

The pillar guide on [crypto vs stocks](/investing/crypto-vs-stocks/) covers how asset choice, risk and time horizon shape returns. Dollar cost averaging sits inside that bigger picture. It decides how money enters an investment, not which investment it enters or how much risk that investment carries.

Seen that way, the statistics are less dramatic than headlines suggest. A lump sum has usually earned a little more when cash is ready. A regular schedule has usually beaten waiting in cash, and it fits the way most people earn and save.

### What the Research Cannot Predict

Vanguard's results describe 1976 to 2022 and a set of simulations. They do not say what will happen after any particular start date in 2026. A sharp fall in the months after a lump sum is always possible, and it is exactly the case where a staggered plan wins.

### Reading Future Headlines

A new study or social media chart may claim one method is far better. Check whether it compares the same pool of money, uses total returns, tests many start dates and states its cash rate. Without those details, a dramatic claim is hard to judge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Dollar Cost Averaging Guarantee a Profit?

No. A portfolio can still lose value even when purchases happen at several prices. The method changes when money enters the market, not the risk of the investments themselves.

### Is Dollar Cost Averaging Better Than Lump Sum Investing?

Not usually for money already in hand. Vanguard found a lump sum beat a three-month split about two-thirds of the time from 1976 to 2022, though averaging lowered early regret.

### Is Investing Every Payday Dollar Cost Averaging?

Yes, in the broad sense of fixed regular contributions. It differs from delaying a lump sum you already hold, because future paychecks cannot be invested before you earn them.

### Does Cash Interest Change the Result?

It narrows the gap. With Treasury bill interest on waiting cash, Vanguard found a lump sum still beat the three-month plan 65% of the time for an all-equity portfolio.

### How Long Should a Phase-In Period Be?

Vanguard found longer phase-in periods cost more expected return. Investors who choose to stagger often use a short fixed schedule set in advance, though the right length depends on circumstances.
