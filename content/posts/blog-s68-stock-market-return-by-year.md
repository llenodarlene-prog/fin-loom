# Stock Market Return by Year: S&P 500 Data From 2008 to 2025 and Where 2026 Stands

A table of yearly stock returns looks simple until two websites show different numbers for the same year. Often neither is wrong. One may report the change in an index's price, another may include reinvested dividends, and a third may use a fund rather than the index itself.

Those differences shrink once you know which definition you are reading. This page gives a stock market return by year table for the S&P 500 from 2008 to 2025, using one consistent source that includes dividends. It adds long-run averages since 1928, explains why 2026 cannot yet be added to the table, and shows what the yearly figures can and cannot tell an investor. Data for 2026 runs through the November 6, 2026 close.

## Key Takeaways

- **One consistent source:** the table uses the S&P 500 annual returns, including dividends, published by Aswath Damodaran of NYU Stern and updated January 5, 2026.
- **Wide swings between years:** returns ranged from -36.55% in 2008 to +32.15% in 2013 over 2008 to 2025.
- **Long-run average:** the simple average from 1928 to 2025 was about 11.9% a year, and the compound average about 10.0%, by our calculation from that dataset.
- **Negative years happen:** 26 of the 98 years from 1928 to 2025 had a negative total return in that series.
- **2026 is not finished:** Janus Henderson reported the S&P 500 up 11.7% year to date through October, before a 4.4% one-day fall on November 5.

## What Are Stock Market Returns by Year?

A calendar-year return measures performance from the last trading day of one year to the last trading day of the next. A price return measures only the change in the index level. A total return adds dividends, usually assuming they are reinvested. A real return adjusts the result for inflation.

For long-term reference, a consistent total-return series is more useful than a mix of charts. The table below uses Damodaran's S&P 500 series, which includes dividends. Figures from other providers can differ by small amounts because of data sources, dividend timing or rounding.

### Price Return Versus Total Return

Dividends add a meaningful amount over time. In a year when the index level rises 10% and dividends add about 1.5%, the total return is roughly 11.5%. Over decades, reinvested dividends make a large difference to the ending value, which is why total-return series are the better long-term guide.

### Nominal Versus Real Returns

A nominal return is the raw percentage change. A real return subtracts inflation, showing how much purchasing power grew. A 10% nominal return in a year with 4% inflation is about a 6% real return. The table below is nominal.

### What the 2008 to 2025 Period Shows

Over these 18 years, the simple average of the yearly returns was about 12.6%, and the compound annual rate was about 11.0%, by our calculation from the table. Three years were negative: 2008, 2018 and 2022. Fifteen were positive, and nine of those gained more than 15%.

### Why Other Tables Show Slightly Different Numbers

Another provider might show -37.0% for 2008 or 26.3% for 2023. Small gaps like these usually come from different dividend data, reinvestment timing or index versions. They are not errors. The safest approach is to use one named series for any comparison and say which one you used.

## Long-Run Averages Since 1928

Damodaran's dataset reaches back to 1928. By our calculation from that series, the S&P 500's simple average annual return from 1928 to 2025 was about 11.9%, and its compound annual growth rate was about 10.0%. Over those 98 years, 26 had a negative total return.

The two averages answer different questions. The simple average adds up each year's return and divides by the number of years. The compound rate is the steady yearly return that would turn the same starting amount into the same ending amount. The compound rate is lower because losses hurt more than equal gains help.

### Why the Compound Rate Is Lower

A 50% loss followed by a 50% gain does not bring you back to where you started. $100 falls to $50, then rises to $75. The simple average of -50% and +50% is zero, but the investor is down 25%. That gap is why long-term planning usually relies on compound averages.

### How Often Stocks Fell in a Calendar Year

In the 1928 to 2025 series, roughly one year in four had a negative total return. Some losses were small, such as -4.23% in 2018. Others were severe, such as -43.84% in 1931 and -36.55% in 2008. The average conceals that range.

## Why 2026 Is Different From a Completed Year

As of the November 6, 2026 close, 2026 was not a completed calendar year. Its result cannot fairly be placed beside finished years without a clear year-to-date label. Janus Henderson reported the S&P 500 was up 11.7% year to date through the end of October, and the equal-weight S&P 500 was up 15.5%.

Then the market fell sharply. FRED's [S&P 500 series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500) shows the index closing at 7,353.73 on November 5, 2026, down about 4.4% from 7,688.62 the day before. That single session cut into the year-to-date gain, and the full-year figure will depend on what happens through December 31.

### Year-to-Date Is Not a Forecast

A year with a sharp drawdown can still end positive. An early strong gain cannot rule out a negative year-end. The 2020 example shows this: Damodaran's series records a +18.02% total return for 2020, a year that included a severe crash in its first quarter.

### How to Label a Partial Year

Any 2026 figure should state its end date and definition, for example "S&P 500 price return, January 1 to November 6, 2026." Without those labels, a partial-year number can be mistaken for a full-year result and compared unfairly with history.

### What the Decade View Adds

The decade view shows that strong and weak periods tend to come in runs rather than alternating evenly. It also shows that the 2020 to 2025 period, despite a pandemic crash and a negative 2022, compounded at about 14.9% a year in this series.

### Limits of the Decade View

Decades are arbitrary windows. Starting a period one year earlier or later can change the result considerably, especially around large moves such as 2008 or 2009. Use decade figures for context, not as evidence that a particular decade will repeat.

## What the Yearly Distribution Teaches

The table shows wide differences between neighboring years: -36.55% in 2008 then +25.94% in 2009, and -18.04% in 2022 then +26.06% in 2023. These do not form a reliable alternating pattern. The market is not obliged to deliver a strong year after every weak one.

A single annual figure also hides the path an investor lived through. Two years with similar December-to-December results can contain very different declines along the way. For someone withdrawing money during the year, the order of gains and losses can affect results as much as the annual average.

### Sequence of Returns

Two investors with the same average return can end up with different results if one faces losses early while withdrawing money. Losses early in retirement, for example, reduce the balance that later gains can grow. This is called sequence-of-returns risk, and annual tables alone do not show it.

### Crashes Inside Good Years

Annual returns do not show how far prices fell within a year. FinLoom's [stock market crash history](/markets-stocks/stock-market-crash-history-statistics/) separates one-day crashes, multi-year bear markets and full-year results, which are often mixed up in tables like this one. That context helps explain why a positive year can still contain a frightening decline.

## What About Inflation and Fees?

A nominal total return is not a measure of growth in purchasing power. Inflation reduces what each future dollar buys. Fund expense ratios, trading costs and taxes reduce what an investor keeps. The table describes an unmanaged index, not a real account after costs.

That is why a phrase like "the market returns 10% a year" is incomplete. It could mean a long-run compound average, a single year's result, a price return or a rate assumed in a calculator. Those are different numbers and should not be treated as forecasts.

### Inflation in Practice

Inflation varies a great deal from year to year. A stock market return by year table that shows a 10% gain in a year of 8% inflation describes far less real growth than the same 10% gain in a year of 2% inflation. Comparing nominal returns across decades without adjusting for inflation can overstate how much purchasing power grew.

### A Simple Fee Example

On a $10,000 balance growing at a hypothetical 10% for one year, a fund charging 0.03% a year costs about $3, and a fund charging 1.00% costs about $100, before any market changes. These are illustrative figures. Over many years, higher fees compound into large differences in ending value.

## How to Use a Stock Market Return by Year Table

A yearly table is useful for understanding the range of outcomes, not for predicting next year. It shows that strong years, weak years and severe losses all occur, and that the long-run average sits between very different individual results.

Use the table to set expectations about volatility, then base decisions on time horizon, need for cash and tolerance for losses. FinLoom's [buy the dip statistics](/investing/buy-the-dip-statistics/) explain why yearly averages cannot identify the best week or month to invest. FinLoom's guide to [stock analysis fundamentals](/markets-stocks/stock-analysis-fundamentals/) covers company-level analysis that index tables leave out.

### Matching Data to the Question

Use completed calendar years for historical comparisons. Use year-to-date figures, clearly labeled, for the current year. Use compound averages for long-term planning. Use peak-to-trough declines, not annual returns, to understand how bad a downturn felt along the way for someone who held throughout.

### Using the Table Alongside a Plan

A written plan sets a target mix of stocks, bonds and cash based on goals and time horizon. The stock market return by year table helps test that plan against history: would you have stayed invested through 2008 or 2022? If not, the plan may carry more stock risk than you can tolerate in practice.

## How the Best and Worst Years Compare

Looking at extremes shows the range an investor in the S&P 500 has faced. In Damodaran's series, the worst calendar year from 1928 to 2025 was 1931, at -43.84%, followed by 2008 at -36.55%. The 1930s also included -25.12% in 1930.

Strong years have been just as large. Within 2008 to 2025, the best year was 2013 at +32.15%, followed by 2019 at +31.21%. Any stock market return by year table that spans several decades will show both kinds of extremes, which is why long-run averages need to be read alongside the full range.

### Why Extremes Cluster

Large losses and large gains often sit close together in time. After 2008's loss came 2009's gain of 25.94%. After 2022's loss came 2023's gain of 26.06%. That pattern reflects how markets reprice after shocks, not a rule that every loss is followed by a recovery the next year.

### What This Means for Planning

Planning around the average alone can understate risk. A plan that only works if every year returns 10% will fail in practice, because single years have ranged from about -44% to more than +30%. Building in room for bad years makes a plan more durable and easier to stick with over time.

## Common Mistakes With Stock Market Return by Year Data

The first mistake is mixing price returns and total returns in one table. The second is placing an incomplete year beside completed years without a label. The third is treating the simple average as the growth rate an investor would have earned, when the compound rate is the better measure.

The fourth is reading patterns into the sequence of years, such as expecting a gain after every loss. The fifth is assuming different providers are wrong when their figures differ slightly. Naming the source and definition for every stock market return by year figure avoids most of these problems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are S&P 500 Returns the Same as Total Stock Market Returns?

No. The S&P 500 tracks about 500 large U.S. companies. Total-market benchmarks include small and mid-sized companies, so their yearly returns can differ.

### Does This Table Include Dividends?

Yes. It uses Damodaran's S&P 500 annual return series, which includes dividends. Price-only tables will show lower returns in most years.

### What Is the Average Stock Market Return per Year?

From 1928 to 2025, the S&P 500's simple average was about 11.9% and its compound rate about 10.0%, by our calculation from Damodaran's series.

### Is the 2026 Return Final?

No. As of November 6, 2026, the year is unfinished. Janus Henderson reported the S&P 500 up 11.7% through October, before a sharp November 5 decline.
