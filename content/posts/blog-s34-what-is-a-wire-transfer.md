# What Is a Wire Transfer? How Bank Wires Work

What is a wire transfer? It is an electronic payment in which one financial institution sends a payment instruction to another, so money moves from one account to another, often within the same business day. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-wire-transfer-en-1163/) describes a wire transfer as a common way to electronically move money from one bank account to another, either between two U.S. accounts or between a U.S. account and an international one.

In the United States, many domestic wires settle through the Federal Reserve's Fedwire Funds Service. Consumers do not connect to Fedwire themselves. Their bank or credit union sends or receives the wire for them, and once a Fedwire payment is processed, it is final.

## Key Takeaways

- A wire transfer sends a payment instruction electronically between financial institutions.
- The CFPB describes wires as a common way to move money electronically between bank accounts, at home or abroad.
- Fedwire, owned and operated by the Federal Reserve Banks, is a major U.S. system for domestic wires.
- Fedwire settles each payment individually, and payments are final and irrevocable once made.
- In September 2026, Fedwire handled 19,786,425 transfers worth about $105.15 trillion.
- Banks can charge separate fees for outgoing, incoming, and international wires.
- A completed wire is hard to recover if it reaches a scammer or the wrong account.
- Some international consumer transfers carry federal cancellation and error-resolution rights.

## What Is a Wire Transfer in Plain Terms?

A wire transfer is a bank-to-bank payment instruction. The sender asks their bank to move a set amount to a named recipient at another institution. The sending bank checks the request, debits the sender's account, and passes the instruction through a wire network. The receiving bank then credits the recipient.

The word "wire" comes from the telegraph era, but no physical cash travels anywhere today. What moves is an electronic message, followed by settlement between the two banks. That is why a wire can move a very large amount quickly: the banks settle the payment between themselves, then post it to customer accounts.

The answer to what is a wire transfer also depends on where the money goes. The CFPB separates domestic wires, which move funds between two U.S. accounts, from international wires, which move money between a U.S. account and a foreign one. International consumer transfers can also fall under extra federal rules, described in the remittance section below.

## How a Wire Transfer Works Step by Step

A typical domestic wire follows the same basic path at most banks. The exact screens and forms differ, but the sequence below describes what happens behind them:

- **Request:** The sender gives the bank the recipient's details and the amount, online, by phone, or at a branch.
- **Verification:** The bank confirms the sender's identity and authority over the account.
- **Screening:** The bank runs fraud and compliance checks on the payment.
- **Instruction:** The bank formats a payment message and submits it to a wire system such as Fedwire.
- **Settlement:** The system debits the sending bank's account and credits the receiving bank's account.
- **Posting:** The receiving bank credits its customer under its own procedures.

Most of the time a sender sees only the first step and a confirmation. The screening and posting steps explain why a wire can still take some hours even though the settlement itself is fast.

## What Is Fedwire and Who Runs It?

The Federal Reserve's [Fedwire Funds Service business continuity guide](https://www.frbservices.org/financial-services/fedwire-funds-service/fedwire-funds-service-business-continuity-guide/) describes Fedwire as a real-time gross settlement (RTGS) system owned and operated by the Federal Reserve Banks. The same guide says over 5,000 participants may send and receive time-sensitive payments through it, and that payments are made in central bank money and are final and irrevocable once made.

Federal Reserve Financial Services describes the service as one that institutions rely on for mission-critical, same-day transactions.

### Who Uses Fedwire

Participants use Fedwire to send or receive payments for their own accounts or for corporate and individual clients. They also use it to settle commercial payments, settle positions with other financial institutions, submit federal tax payments, and buy and sell federal funds. Consumer wires are only one part of that traffic, which helps explain the very large dollar values shown later.

### What Real-Time Gross Settlement Means

"Real-time" means an accepted payment is processed when it arrives during operating hours, rather than waiting for a scheduled batch. "Gross settlement" means each payment settles on its own, instead of being netted against other payments first. If one bank sends another a $10 million payment, that payment settles as a single item.

## Wire Transfers Move Enormous Dollar Values

Fedwire carries fewer payments than consumer payment systems, but each payment is usually large. The [September 2026 Fedwire statistics](https://www.frbservices.org/wp-content/uploads/102026-monthly-september-funds-ascii.txt) published by Federal Reserve Financial Services show these figures for the month:

| Fedwire Measure, September 2026 | Value |
|---|---:|
| Transfers originated | 19,786,425 |
| Total value | About $105.15 trillion |
| Average value per transfer | About $5.31 million |
| Average daily value | About $5.01 trillion |

These totals include wholesale and interbank payments, not only consumer wires. The average transfer of more than $5 million reflects that institutional use.

### A Year of Fedwire Volume

Adding the monthly rows for 2025 in the same Federal Reserve file gives about 217.3 million transfers worth roughly $1.148 quadrillion for the year. That is a Fin Loom sum of the published monthly figures, not a separately published annual total. It shows how much of the U.S. financial system relies on wire settlement every business day.

### Why Averages Mislead for Consumers

A $5.31 million average does not mean a consumer wire needs to be large. The average is pulled up by interbank and corporate transfers. A person can wire a few hundred dollars, although the fee may make a wire a poor fit for small routine payments.

## What Information Does a Wire Transfer Need?

Every bank sets its own form, so the sending bank's instructions are the final word on required fields. Most wires need the same core details, and international wires add a few more.

### Details for a Domestic Wire

A domestic wire usually needs the recipient's full name, the recipient's bank name, the account number, the bank's ABA routing number, and the amount. Some banks also ask for the recipient's address and a short payment reference, such as an invoice or closing file number. Errors in any of these fields can delay or misdirect the payment.

### Details for an International Wire

An international wire often adds the receiving bank's SWIFT or BIC code, an IBAN for countries that use one, the beneficiary's address, the currency, and sometimes intermediary bank details and a purpose-of-payment code. Because more banks and rules are involved, a missing field abroad is more likely to stop the payment for manual review.

## Routing Numbers, SWIFT Codes, and IBANs Are Different

These identifiers do different jobs, and mixing them up is a common cause of failed wires. Each one points to a different part of the payment path:

- **ABA Routing Number:** Identifies a U.S. financial institution for domestic payments.
- **SWIFT or BIC Code:** Identifies a financial institution in international payment messages.
- **IBAN:** Identifies a specific account under the banking format of countries that use it.

A SWIFT code points to a bank, while an IBAN points to an account at that bank. A U.S. domestic wire normally relies on the routing number and the account number instead.

## How Long Does a Domestic Wire Take?

A domestic wire often arrives the same business day when the sender submits it before the bank's cutoff. The Federal Reserve's published operating hours say the Fedwire funds-transfer business day begins at 9:00 p.m. ET on the preceding calendar day and ends at 7:00 p.m. ET, with a 6:45 p.m. ET deadline for core customer transfers. A consumer's own bank can set an earlier cutoff.

The full set of timing factors, including weekends, holidays, and compliance holds, is covered in [how long does a wire transfer take](/payments/how-long-does-a-wire-transfer-take/), which also explains what a "pending" status can mean.

### Why International Wires Can Take Longer

An international wire can pass through the sending bank, one or more correspondent or intermediary banks, a currency conversion step, sanctions screening, and the receiving country's own banking system. Each handoff can add time, and time zones and foreign bank holidays can push delivery past one business day.

### What Can Slow Any Wire

Even a domestic wire can wait for identity checks, fraud review, sanctions screening, or correction of beneficiary details. A payment that misses the bank's cutoff usually moves on the next business day. These delays come from the banks' own controls, not from the speed of the settlement system.

## Why Wire Transfers Are Hard to Reverse

Fedwire payments are final and irrevocable once made, according to the Federal Reserve's business continuity guide. That finality is why large transactions, such as home purchases and corporate payments, often use wires: once the funds settle, the receiving bank can rely on them.

The same feature carries a cost for senders. If money goes to the wrong account or to a fraudster, the sending bank can ask the receiving bank to return it, but a return request does not guarantee recovery. The recipient or the receiving institution may need to cooperate.

### What to Do After a Mistaken Wire

Contact the sending bank at once and give it the payment details. If the bank has not yet released the instruction, it may still be able to stop it. After settlement, the bank can only request a return. Acting quickly gives the banks the best chance to locate the funds before they move again.

### How Finality Differs From Card Payments

A card payment can often be disputed through the card issuer. A completed wire has no comparable chargeback process at the settlement level. That is why wire instructions deserve more checking than many other payments, especially for large amounts that would be hard to replace.

## Wire Fraud and Business Email Compromise

Many wire losses come from changed payment instructions rather than from any fault in the settlement system. The system can work exactly as designed while sending money to the wrong person.

### Common Fraud Patterns

Typical schemes include fake real estate closing instructions, compromised vendor email accounts, impersonated executives asking for urgent payments, altered invoices, and requests to "update" bank details. Each one tries to get the sender to approve a real wire to an account the criminal controls, usually under time pressure.

### Verifying Instructions Before Sending

For an important wire, confirm the account details through a phone number or contact method already known to be genuine, not one supplied in the same email. Treat any last-minute change to bank details as a warning sign. Banks and title companies repeat this advice because a completed wire is so hard to recover.

## Remittance Protections for International Consumer Wires

Some international transfers sent by consumers count as remittance transfers under federal rules. The CFPB says that, when the rules apply, the provider generally must disclose fees and taxes, the exchange rate, the amount to be delivered, when the money will be available, the sender's right to cancel, and what to do in case of an error.

### The 30-Minute Cancellation Window

Under the CFPB's remittance rules, a sender generally has up to 30 minutes to cancel a covered transfer after paying for it, unless the money has already been picked up or deposited. This right applies to covered international remittances, not to every domestic bank wire, so senders should not assume it covers a routine U.S. payment.

### Reporting an Error

The CFPB says a sender has 180 days to report an error on a covered remittance, starting from the date the provider disclosed as the date the money would be available. Remedies can include a refund or having the transfer resent, depending on the error. These rights sit alongside, not inside, Fedwire's settlement finality.

## How a Wire Transfer Compares With ACH

ACH and wires are separate payment rails. ACH payments move in batches and handle payroll, bills, and recurring transfers, while wires settle individually and suit larger or urgent payments. The trade-offs in speed, cost, and reversibility are laid out in [ACH vs wire transfer](/payments/ach-vs-wire-transfer/), which uses Q3 2026 Nacha data and the same Fedwire statistics.

For many readers, the practical takeaway is that a wire trades lower flexibility for faster, final settlement. ACH is usually cheaper and has established return processes, but it is less suited to a payment that must land with certainty on a set day.

## What Does a Wire Transfer Cost?

Wire fees are set by each financial institution, so the only reliable figure is the one on a bank's own fee schedule. A bank may charge for some or all of these:

- **Outgoing Domestic Wires:** A fee for sending a wire within the United States.
- **Incoming Wires:** A fee some banks charge to receive a wire.
- **Outgoing International Wires:** Usually higher than domestic fees.
- **Currency Conversion:** A cost built into the exchange rate when currencies change.
- **Intermediary Charges:** Fees taken by correspondent banks along the route.

Some premium accounts waive selected wire fees. Because fee schedules change, checking the current schedule before sending avoids surprises, especially for international wires where several parties can take a share.

## Fedwire and SWIFT Are Not the Same System

Fedwire is a U.S. funds-transfer and settlement system. SWIFT is a global financial messaging network that banks use to send payment instructions between institutions. An international payment can travel as a SWIFT message, while the actual settlement happens through correspondent bank accounts or a national system.

That is why the names are not interchangeable. A U.S. domestic wire may settle on Fedwire without any SWIFT message, and a SWIFT message alone does not move money between banks. When a bank quotes an international wire, it is often describing both the message and the settlement route.

## Where Wires Fit Among Other Ways to Pay

Wires are one option among several. Consumers also use ACH transfers, cards, instant payment services, and digital wallets for everyday payments. Digital wallets have grown into large payment businesses in their own right, a shift visible in [PayPal statistics](/payments/paypal-statistics/) on accounts and payment volume.

The answer to what is a wire transfer, then, is less about speed alone and more about certainty. A wire is built for payments where the sender and recipient both need the money to settle individually, quickly, and with finality, and where the fee is small relative to the amount.

### When a Wire Is Often Used

Wires are common for real estate closings, large business payments, time-sensitive account funding, and some international bank-to-bank transfers. In each case the value of certain, same-day settlement tends to outweigh the fee. Smaller routine payments usually travel on cheaper rails such as ACH.

### When Another Method May Fit Better

Recurring bills, payroll, and small person-to-person payments rarely need wire finality. For those, ACH or an instant payment service can cost less and offer more ways to fix mistakes. Matching the payment method to the amount, urgency, and risk is more useful than treating any one rail as the best.

## What Is a Wire Transfer Best Suited For?

What is a wire transfer best at? Moving a known amount, to a verified recipient, with fast and final settlement. Those same strengths make checking the recipient's details the most important step for the sender.

Understanding what is a wire transfer, how Fedwire settles it, and which protections do or do not apply helps a sender judge when a wire is worth its fee. The sending bank's own cutoffs, fees, and forms remain the final source for any specific payment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is a Wire Transfer in Simple Terms?

A wire transfer is an electronic bank-to-bank payment instruction that moves money between accounts, often for urgent, large, or international payments, with settlement usually completed the same day.

### Is a Wire Transfer Immediate?

Fedwire processes accepted payments in real time, but arrival also depends on bank cutoffs, fraud checks, business days, receiving-bank posting, and whether the wire is domestic or international.

### Can a Wire Transfer Be Reversed?

A completed Fedwire payment is final and irrevocable. The sending bank can request a return, but recovery depends on the receiving side and is not guaranteed after settlement.

### Is a Wire Transfer the Same as ACH?

No. ACH is a batch-based network for routine credits and debits, while wires settle each payment individually and are typically used for larger or time-sensitive transfers.

### Is SWIFT the Same as a Wire Transfer?

No. SWIFT is a global messaging network that carries payment instructions between banks. Settlement happens separately, through correspondent accounts or a national system such as Fedwire.
