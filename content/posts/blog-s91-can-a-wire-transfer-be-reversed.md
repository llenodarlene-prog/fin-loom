# Can a Wire Transfer Be Reversed? Cancellation, Recall and Error Rights

Can a wire transfer be reversed? Usually not once it has gone through. Wires are built for speed and finality, which is why they are used for home closings and other large payments. That same design makes mistakes and scams hard to undo.

There are some exceptions and some steps worth taking right away. International transfers sent by consumers can carry a short cancellation right under federal rules. Errors can be disputed in some cases. A bank can also ask the receiving bank to return funds, though it cannot force the return. This guide explains each path using Federal Reserve and Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) sources reviewed on November 21, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Fedwire payments are final once processed:** the Federal Reserve calls them immediate, final and irrevocable.
- **Regulation E excludes Fedwire-type wires:** the consumer transfer rule does not cover transfers through Fedwire or similar systems used mainly between institutions or businesses.
- **International consumer transfers have a 30-minute window:** CFPB rules let senders cancel a remittance transfer within 30 minutes of paying, if conditions are met.
- **Refunds have a deadline:** a provider must refund a properly canceled remittance within three business days.
- **Errors have a longer window:** senders can report certain remittance errors up to 180 days after the disclosed availability date.
- **Speed is the main tool:** contact your bank at once, ask for a recall, and report fraud to the FBI's IC3.

## Can a Wire Transfer Be Reversed After It Is Sent?

So can a wire transfer be reversed once it is complete? In most cases, a completed wire cannot be pulled back by the sender. The [Federal Reserve's description of the Fedwire Funds Service](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fedfunds_about.htm) says it is a real-time gross settlement system that lets participants start transfers that are immediate, final and irrevocable once processed.

That finality is the point of the system. A seller in a large transaction wants to know the money will not disappear later. The trade-off falls on the sender. If the wire went to the wrong account or to a scammer, the sender cannot simply cancel it the way a card charge can sometimes be disputed.

### How a Wire Moves

The Federal Reserve describes Fedwire as a credit transfer service. Participants send a transfer by telling a Federal Reserve Bank to debit funds from their own account and credit the account of another participant. Banks can start transfers online, by secure electronic message, or by telephone procedures.

The Fed lists the Fedwire business day as starting at 9:00 p.m. Eastern time on the previous calendar day and ending at 7:00 p.m. Eastern, Monday through Friday, excluding holidays. The deadline for transfers on behalf of a third party, such as a bank's customer, is 6:45 p.m. Eastern. The [how long does a wire transfer take](/payments/how-long-does-a-wire-transfer-take/) guide covers timing in more detail.

### Why Timing Is So Important

Because transfers are final once processed, the only reliable time to stop a wire is before the bank sends it. If you spot a mistake while the wire is still pending at your bank, calling right away may let the bank halt it. Once it is processed, the options shift from cancellation to recovery.

That is why banks often require extra verification for large wires and why scammers push for urgency. A request to wire money quickly, especially with new instructions, is a common warning sign. Pausing to verify instructions by calling a known phone number can prevent a loss that later rules cannot fix.

## Domestic Wires and Consumer Protection Rules

Many people assume consumer payment protections cover wires. For domestic wires, they generally do not. The CFPB's Regulation E, in section 1005.3, excludes any transfer of funds through Fedwire or through a similar wire transfer system used primarily for transfers between financial institutions or between businesses.

Regulation E is the rule that gives consumers dispute rights for many electronic transfers, such as debit card payments and ACH transfers. With wires carved out, a consumer who sends a domestic wire usually relies on the bank's account agreement and other applicable law rather than Regulation E's error and liability rules.

### How Wires Compare With ACH

ACH transfers work differently. They move in batches and have their own rules for returns in some circumstances. The [ACH vs wire transfer](/payments/ach-vs-wire-transfer/) comparison sets out the main differences in speed, cost and reversibility between the two systems.

That difference shapes how people choose a payment method. ACH can be slower but more forgiving in some cases. Wires are faster and final. For high-value payments where finality is the goal, a wire suits the purpose. For payments where a mistake is more likely, the reversal options tilt toward ACH.

### What Your Bank Agreement Says

Since Regulation E does not cover domestic wires, the bank's wire transfer agreement is the main document. It usually explains cutoff times, how to request a cancellation before processing, and what the bank will do if you ask for a recall. Reading it before sending a large wire shows what help to expect.

The agreement may also explain security procedures, such as callbacks or codes, used to confirm wire instructions. Because terms differ by bank, asking your bank directly is the most reliable way to understand your position.

## International Transfers: The 30-Minute Cancellation Right

International transfers sent by consumers get stronger protection. The CFPB's remittance rules give senders a right to cancel. The CFPB's answer to [can I cancel an international money transfer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-i-cancel-an-international-money-transfer-en-1737/) says you may be able to cancel, depending on the circumstances.

According to the CFPB, you have the right to cancel at no cost if you paid for the transfer less than 30 minutes ago and you gave enough information for the provider to find the transfer. If you scheduled the transfer in advance, you may cancel up to three business days before it is made. State law or the provider's own rules may give more time.

| Situation | Cancellation Right Under CFPB Rules |
|---|---|
| International transfer paid less than 30 minutes ago | Can cancel at no cost if the provider can identify it |
| Scheduled international transfer | Can cancel up to three business days before it is made |
| International transfer paid more than 30 minutes ago | Federal cancellation right has passed; error rights may apply |
| Domestic wire through Fedwire | Not covered by Regulation E; depends on bank terms |

The answer to can a wire transfer be reversed changes row by row, depending on where the money went and how much time has passed.

### Reading the Cancellation Table

The table shows how much depends on the type of transfer and the clock. An international transfer caught within 30 minutes has a clear federal path. A domestic wire has no matching federal right. The middle rows show that timing turns a cancellation right into a narrower set of options.

The 30-minute window is short. If you realize a mistake on an international transfer, contacting the provider immediately gives the best chance. The CFPB advises reviewing your contract and receipt and contacting the company immediately when you want to cancel.

### What the Regulation Requires

Regulation E section 1005.34 sets the details. It says a remittance transfer provider must honor an oral or written cancellation request received no later than 30 minutes after the sender pays, if the conditions are met. The official interpretation adds that the provider may offer a longer cancellation period.

The interpretation also says the provider must offer the 30-minute right regardless of its normal business hours. Once a valid cancellation is made, the regulation says the provider must refund the total amount the sender provided, including fees and, where not prohibited by law, taxes, within three business days of receiving the request.

## Error Rights for Remittance Transfers

After the cancellation window, error rights may still apply to international consumer transfers. Regulation E section 1005.33 covers errors such as an incorrect amount of currency received or a failure to make funds available by the disclosed date. These are specific categories, not a general right to undo a payment.

The time limit is longer than the cancellation window. Section 1005.33 sets a notice window of 180 days from the disclosed date of availability, with an extension in some cases if the provider requested more information. Keeping the receipt and the disclosed availability date makes it easier to file within the window.

### What Counts as an Error

The regulation lists types of errors with detailed examples. Paying the wrong amount, the recipient receiving less than disclosed, and funds not arriving by the disclosed date are among them. The rules also explain cases that are not errors, such as some failures caused by extraordinary circumstances outside the provider's control.

Sending money to a scammer who received it as instructed is generally not the kind of error these rules address. The rules focus on the provider's handling of the transfer, not on whether the sender was deceived. That is why fraud cases rely more on fast reporting and recall requests than on error rights.

### How to File an Error Notice

Contact the provider in writing or by phone and describe the transfer and the problem. Include the date, amount, recipient and receipt details. Ask how the provider handles error notices and keep a record of every contact, including names, dates and reference numbers.

If the provider does not resolve the issue, keep your records and ask what escalation options it offers. A written record of each contact supports any later complaint and shows that the notice was filed inside the 180-day window.

## What a Wire Recall Means

When people ask can a wire transfer be reversed, they often mean a recall. A recall is a request, not a reversal. When a sender asks their bank to recall a wire, the sending bank contacts the receiving bank and asks it to return the funds. The receiving bank may need the recipient's permission, and the money may already be gone. Success depends heavily on speed.

Because Fedwire transfers are final once processed, a recall cannot force funds back through the system. It relies on cooperation from the receiving bank and, often, the recipient.

### Steps to Take Right Away

If a wire went to the wrong place or to a scammer, act within minutes, not days. Call your bank's fraud or wire department using a number you trust, such as the one on your card or statement. Ask the bank to recall the wire and to contact the receiving bank. Write down the time, the person you spoke with and any reference number.

Next, report fraud to the FBI. The [FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov/) describes itself as the central hub for reporting cyber-enabled crime and encourages people to file a report even if they are unsure whether the complaint qualifies. IC3 says it cannot guarantee a response to every complaint.

### How Common Wire Fraud Losses Are

IC3's site shows loss data reported for 2020 to 2024 and says that over $50 billion was reported lost over that period. That total covers many types of cyber-enabled crime, not just wire fraud. It still shows why scammers favor fast, final payment methods.

Payment fraud spans many channels. The [PayPal statistics](/payments/paypal-statistics/) guide looks at one large digital payment network, which operates under different rules from wires. Comparing how each payment method handles disputes helps explain why wires are a favorite target when scammers want money that cannot be recalled.

## How to Prevent a Wire You Cannot Undo

Prevention works better than recovery. Before sending a wire, confirm the recipient's account details by calling a phone number you already know is genuine, not one in the email or text asking for the payment. Scammers often send fake instructions that look like they come from a title company, employer or relative.

Be cautious with last-minute changes to payment instructions. A message saying the account has changed, especially near a closing date or deadline, is a common sign of fraud. Taking a few minutes to verify can prevent a loss that no rule can reverse once the wire is processed.

### Verifying Payment Instructions

The safest verification uses contact details you already had before the payment request arrived. An old email thread, a signed contract or a phone number from an official website are better sources than anything in a new message. If the details do not match, stop and ask questions before any money moves, since no one can promise that a wire can be reversed later.

### Questions to Ask Before Sending

A short checklist helps. Who requested the wire, and how did you verify the request? Do the recipient's name and account match what you expected? Is there urgency or secrecy in the request? Does your bank offer a callback or a delay for large first-time wires? Answering each one before sending lowers the risk.

Banks often add their own checks for large or unusual wires, because the honest answer to can a wire transfer be reversed is usually no. Those checks can feel slow, but they exist because recovery is so difficult. Working with the bank's verification steps, rather than around them, gives the sender the best protection available.

## The Bottom Line

Can a wire transfer be reversed? Once a Fedwire payment is processed, the Federal Reserve calls it final and irrevocable, and Regulation E does not cover domestic wires. International consumer transfers can be canceled within 30 minutes under CFPB rules, with a refund due within three business days, and some errors can be disputed within 180 days. For anything else, act fast: ask your bank for a recall, report fraud to IC3, and verify instructions before you ever send.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a Wire Transfer Be Reversed by My Bank?

Not once a Fedwire payment is processed, since the Federal Reserve calls it final. Your bank can request a recall from the receiving bank, but the return depends on cooperation.

### How Long Do I Have to Cancel an International Transfer?

CFPB rules let consumers cancel within 30 minutes of paying, if the provider can identify the transfer. Scheduled transfers can be canceled up to three business days before.

### Does Regulation E Protect Domestic Wires?

No. Regulation E excludes transfers through Fedwire or similar systems used mainly between financial institutions or businesses. Your bank agreement and other law govern domestic wires.

### What Should I Do If I Wired Money to a Scammer?

Call your bank's fraud department at once and ask for a recall. Then file a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Speed improves the chance of recovery.

### How Long Do I Have to Report a Remittance Error?

Regulation E gives senders 180 days from the disclosed date of availability to report certain remittance errors, with some extensions if the provider requested more information.
