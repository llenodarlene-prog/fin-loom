# Dividend Reinvestment Plan Calculator: How DRIP Compounding Works

This dividend reinvestment plan calculator shows how a dividend reinvestment plan, or DRIP, can change your share count and your dividend income over time. Enter a starting position, a dividend, and a time period, and the calculator works through every payment to show what reinvesting could add under the assumptions you choose.

```calculator
drip
```

A DRIP uses each cash dividend to buy more shares of the same investment instead of paying the dividend out as cash. Those new shares can then earn dividends of their own, so the position compounds for as long as the dividend continues and the shares stay invested. The result is an illustration of the arithmetic, not a forecast of what any stock will do.

## Key Takeaways

- A DRIP reinvests cash dividends into additional shares instead of paying them out.
- New shares earn future dividends, so share count and dividend income can grow together.
- With no growth assumed, 100 shares at $50 paying $0.50 a quarter grow to about 122.019 shares in five years, a Fin Loom illustration.
- Reinvested dividends are still taxable in a taxable account, according to IRS Publication 550.
- Each reinvested purchase starts its own holding period and carries its own cost basis.
- Results depend heavily on assumptions about share price, dividend growth, and whether the dividend is maintained.
- A dividend cut can sharply reduce the projected result, and a high yield does not guarantee a strong total return.

## How Dividend Reinvestment Works

The mechanism is simple enough to follow by hand. Suppose an investor owns 100 shares of a company that pays a quarterly dividend of $0.50 per share. The quarterly dividend is 100 × $0.50, or $50. If the stock trades at $50 when the dividend is reinvested, that $50 buys exactly one more share.

The investor now owns 101 shares. If the dividend stays at $0.50 per share, the next payment is 101 × $0.50, or $50.50. That extra 50 cents is small, but it repeats and builds with every payment period. Over many years, repeated reinvestment can steadily raise both the share count and the dividend income.

### Why the First Payment Looks Different

In this example, only the first payment buys a whole share. The second payment of $50.50 buys 1.01 shares, and every payment after that buys an uneven fraction. That is only possible where the plan or brokerage supports fractional shares, which is why plan terms deserve a careful read before anyone relies on a projection.

### Why Compounding Starts Slowly

Each period's new shares add only a little to the next dividend, so the early years look nearly flat. The effect becomes easier to see over long periods, because the growing share count feeds a larger payment, which buys still more shares. Time does more of the work than any single payment does.

## How This Dividend Reinvestment Plan Calculator Works

A simple compound-interest formula is not enough for this job. Dividend reinvestment depends on two moving parts: the cash dividend and the share price on the day the cash is reinvested. So the dividend reinvestment plan calculator processes every dividend period separately rather than applying one annual rate.

For each period, the calculator follows the same steps:

1. **Dividend:** It multiplies the current share count by the dividend per share.
2. **Contributions:** It adds any outside contributions scheduled for that period.
3. **Price:** It sets the share price for that period from your price-growth assumption.
4. **New Shares:** It divides the reinvested cash by that price.
5. **Share Count:** It adds the new shares to the position.
6. **Dividend Growth:** It raises the dividend per share once a year if you entered a growth rate.

The loop repeats until the selected period ends. Working period by period makes every result auditable, because each year's figures can be traced back to the payments that produced them.

### The Inputs You Control

You can set the starting number of shares, the starting share price, the dividend per share, and how often it is paid: monthly, quarterly, semiannually, or annually. You can also enter an annual dividend growth rate, an annual share-price growth rate, a number of years, an optional monthly contribution, and whether dividends are reinvested at all.

### The Results It Reports

The calculator reports the ending share count, the ending value of the position, total dividends generated, total dividends reinvested, and the annual dividend income at the end of the period. It also shows total outside contributions, so money you added stays separate from money the investment generated, plus a year-by-year table and a dividend-income chart.

## Example: Five Years of Reinvestment

A flat scenario in the dividend reinvestment plan calculator makes the cleanest worked example. Enter 100 starting shares, a $50 share price, a $0.50 quarterly dividend, no dividend growth, no share-price growth, no extra deposits, five years, and every dividend reinvested. Because the price never moves, each quarter's dividend equals 1% of the position's value.

### Longer Periods Show the Curve

Keeping every other input the same, ten years of quarterly reinvestment produces about 148.886 shares worth about $7,444.32. Twenty years produces about 221.672 shares worth about $11,083.58, with annual dividend income near $443.34. These are illustrative Fin Loom calculations that assume the dividend and price never change, which real companies rarely deliver.

### Why the Example Is Deliberately Simple

Real share prices move, dividends can be raised or cut, and some plans charge fees or buy shares on set dates. The flat example removes those variables so the reinvestment effect is easy to see on its own. The calculator lets you change each assumption instead of treating one fixed result as typical.

## Dividend Growth Can Have a Large Effect

A growing dividend adds a second compounding force. More shares come from reinvestment, and each share also pays more. In the calculator, a dividend growth rate raises the dividend per share once each year, so the cash available to reinvest climbs even before the extra shares are counted.

Using the same flat inputs with 4% annual dividend growth, five years of reinvestment produces about 124.046 shares and annual dividend income near $290.23, compared with $244.04 with no growth. That is a Fin Loom calculation that holds the share price flat. Dividend growth is never guaranteed, and many companies hold or lower their payouts in weaker years.

### What a Dividend Cut Does

The reverse is also true. If a company cuts its dividend, the cash available for reinvestment falls at once, and every later period buys fewer new shares. Projected income can drop sharply even though the investor owns more shares than at the start, which is why modeling zero or negative dividend growth is a sensible test.

### Testing Several Scenarios

Running a dividend reinvestment plan calculator at a few dividend growth rates, including zero, gives a range rather than a single answer. A range is more honest than one projection, because nobody knows how a company's payout will change. Seeing how far apart the results land shows how much of the outcome rests on an assumption.

## Share-Price Growth Has a Mixed Effect

A rising stock price increases the market value of the shares already owned. It also means each dividend buys fewer new shares, because the same cash goes less far at a higher price. Both effects show up in the calculator when you enter a price growth rate.

Using the same flat inputs with 5% annual price growth and no dividend growth, five years of reinvestment ends with about 119.283 shares instead of 122.019, yet the position is worth about $7,611.93 because each share is worth more. That is a Fin Loom illustration. A lower price lets the same dividend buy more shares, but a falling price can also reflect a weaker business, so lower prices are not automatically good news.

## DRIPs Can Use Fractional Shares

Many brokerage reinvestment programs can buy fractional shares. That lets the full cash dividend go to work even when it is smaller than the price of one whole share. Without fractional purchases, leftover cash may be held or paid out, and the compounding effect shrinks.

Company-sponsored plans can follow different rules from brokerage programs. Some charge fees, some buy shares only on scheduled dates, and some use an average market price rather than a price the investor picks. Reading the plan or brokerage terms before relying on a projection is the practical way to know which assumptions apply.

## Reinvested Dividends Can Still Be Taxable

Automatic reinvestment does not make a taxable dividend tax-free. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) says that if you use your dividends to buy more stock at a price equal to its fair market value, you must still report the dividends as income. The cash never reaches your bank account, but the tax obligation is the same.

Publication 550 also explains that when dividends are reinvested in new shares, the holding period of each new share begins the day after that share was purchased. That detail affects whether a later sale counts as short-term or long-term. Treatment can differ inside tax-advantaged accounts, so the account type is part of the picture.

### Why Tax Is Not in the Calculator

A dividend reinvestment plan calculator like this one does not deduct taxes, because tax depends on the account, the investor's income, and whether each dividend counts as qualified. The rules for [qualified dividends](/investing/qualified-dividends/) decide whether a payment gets the lower capital-gains rates. A pre-tax projection in a taxable account will overstate what an investor keeps.

### Discounted DRIP Purchases

Some company plans let shareholders buy stock below market value. Publication 550 says that if you receive stock from the corporation at a discount, your basis is the full fair market value of the stock on the dividend payment date, and you must include the amount of the discount in your income. The discount is a benefit, but it is also reportable.

## Cost Basis Becomes More Complicated With Reinvestment

Every reinvested dividend buys shares at a new price, and each purchase carries its own cost basis. [IRS Topic 703](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc703) explains that when you buy stocks or bonds, your basis is generally the purchase price plus any additional costs such as commissions. A DRIP turns one purchase into a long series of small ones.

Over many years, a DRIP can create dozens or even hundreds of separate lots. Brokerages often track this information, but the cost basis of the position is not simply the price paid for the original shares. Keeping records of each reinvestment helps when the shares are eventually sold.

## Dividend Yield Alone Is Not Enough

A DRIP compounds only while the dividend stays sustainable. A very high yield can come from a falling share price, shrinking earnings, weak cash flow, heavy debt, or market expectations that the payout will be reduced. A projection that assumes today's dividend continues unchanged can look far better than what the business can support.

That is why the company behind the dividend deserves study before a long projection is trusted. The calculator handles the arithmetic, but it cannot judge whether the payout is safe. Testing a lower dividend growth rate, or a cut, is a way to see how much of the result depends on the business holding up.

### Funds Can Be Reinvested Too

Dividend reinvestment is not limited to individual stocks. Many mutual funds and ETFs let shareholders reinvest distributions, and the choice between an [index fund vs mutual fund](/investing/index-fund-vs-mutual-fund/) structure affects costs and how distributions are taxed. The same period-by-period arithmetic applies to a fund's distributions as to a stock's dividends.

### Where Dividends Fit in a Portfolio

Dividend stocks are one part of a wider portfolio, and their risks differ from other assets. The comparison of [crypto vs stocks](/investing/crypto-vs-stocks/) shows how differently two asset classes can behave over time. A reinvestment plan changes how income is handled, but it does not change the underlying risk of what is owned.

## A DRIP Is Different From a Direct Stock Purchase Plan

The two are easy to confuse. A direct stock purchase plan lets an investor buy shares straight from a company, often with an initial purchase. A dividend reinvestment plan uses dividends from shares already owned to buy more shares. Some programs combine both functions under one plan.

Plan documents spell out the details that change the math: minimum purchases, fees, execution dates, and how shares are sold. Two plans with the same dividend can produce different results once those rules are applied, so the calculator's output is a starting point for comparing terms rather than a final answer.

## Dividend Reinvestment Is a Process, Not a Guarantee

DRIP compounding can raise share count and future dividend income for as long as distributions continue. The mechanism is straightforward, and a dividend reinvestment plan calculator makes it easy to see. The outcome is not straightforward, because it rests on dividend policy, share prices, taxes, fees, and business results.

A useful dividend reinvestment plan calculator separates the arithmetic from the investment case. It shows what could happen under the assumptions you select, and it keeps contributions, dividends, and price changes visible so each one can be judged on its own. Treat every result as an illustration and revisit it when the inputs change.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is a DRIP?

A dividend reinvestment plan automatically uses cash dividends to buy additional shares of the same investment instead of paying the dividend to the investor as cash.

### Are Reinvested Dividends Taxable?

In a taxable account, yes. IRS Publication 550 says dividends used to buy more stock must still be reported as income, and each new share starts its own holding period.

### Does Dividend Reinvestment Increase Returns?

It can increase share count and dividend income, but total return still depends on the share price, dividend sustainability, taxes, fees, and the company's future business results.

### Can a DRIP Buy Fractional Shares?

Many brokerage DRIPs support fractional shares, but rules differ. Company plans and brokerage terms set whether fractional purchases, fees, and specific execution dates apply.

### What Happens If the Dividend Is Cut?

A dividend cut reduces the cash available for reinvestment and can sharply lower projected income. The calculator lets you test lower or zero dividend growth to see the effect.
