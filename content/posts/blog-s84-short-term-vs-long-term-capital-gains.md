# Short Term vs Long Term Capital Gains: Holding Periods and Tax Examples

Two investors can sell the same stock for the same profit and owe very different federal tax. The difference usually comes down to one date: how long each person held the shares. That is the core of short term vs long term capital gains.

The holding period is the starting point, not the whole answer. Taxable income, filing status, capital losses, the type of asset and the 3.8% net investment income tax can all change the final number. This guide explains the IRS rules, shows the 2025 rate thresholds from the IRS, and works through labeled examples. Sources were reviewed on November 20, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **One year is the line:** the IRS treats a gain as long-term if you held the asset for more than one year and short-term if you held it one year or less.
- **Counting starts the day after purchase:** the holding period runs from the day after you acquired the asset up to and including the day you sold it.
- **Short-term gains use ordinary rates:** the IRS says net short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income at graduated rates.
- **Long-term gains can be taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%:** the rate depends on taxable income and filing status, and some assets have higher maximum rates.
- **Losses net first:** capital losses offset gains, and up to $3,000 of excess loss ($1,500 if married filing separately) can reduce other income each year.
- **Another 3.8% can apply:** the net investment income tax reaches some higher-income taxpayers.

## What Is the Difference Between Short Term vs Long Term Capital Gains?

When you sell a capital asset, the difference between your adjusted basis and the amount you realized is a capital gain or loss. [IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) says that if you hold the asset for more than one year before you dispose of it, the gain or loss is long-term. If you hold it one year or less, it is short-term.

The label decides which rate schedule applies. The IRS notes that net short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income at graduated rates. Net capital gain, which is built from long-term results, may qualify for a lower rate. For taxable years beginning in 2025, the IRS says the rate on most net capital gain is no higher than 15% for most individuals.

### How the Holding Period Is Counted

Topic 409 explains the count. You generally start from the day after the day you acquired the asset and count up to and including the day you disposed of it. The words "more than one year" are the key phrase. A sale that lands exactly on the one-year mark is still one year or less under this rule.

A quick example helps. If you bought shares on March 10 of one year, the holding period starts March 11. A sale on March 10 of the following year falls inside one year. A sale on March 11 or later is more than one year. Checking trade confirmations for the exact date avoids a costly one-day mistake.

### Exceptions to the General Rule

Not every asset follows the simple count. Topic 409 points to Publication 544 for property acquired by gift, property acquired from a decedent and patent property. It points to Publication 550 for commodity futures and Publication 541 for applicable partnership interests. These categories can change how the holding period is measured.

Wash sales also move holding periods. When a loss is disallowed under the [wash sale rule](/investing/wash-sale-rule/), IRS Publication 550 says the holding period of the replacement shares includes the period you held the shares you sold. That can turn what looks like a short holding period into a long one.

## Short Term vs Long Term Capital Gains Side by Side

The table below summarizes the general federal treatment for common investment gains, such as stocks and funds held in a taxable account. Collectibles, some real estate gains and certain small business stock follow different maximum rates, which are covered later.

| Feature | Short-Term | Long-Term |
|---|---|---|
| Holding period | One year or less | More than one year |
| Federal rate schedule | Ordinary income rates | 0%, 15% or 20% for most assets |
| How the gain is figured | Amount realized minus adjusted basis | Amount realized minus adjusted basis |
| Loss netting | Netted with short-term results first | Netted with long-term results first |
| Net investment income tax | Can apply | Can apply |
| Reported on | Form 8949 and Schedule D | Form 8949 and Schedule D |

### What Stays the Same

The way the gain is measured does not change with time. Topic 409 defines the gain as the amount realized minus the adjusted basis. A $2,500 profit is a $2,500 profit whether you held the shares for ten months or ten years. Only the rate schedule applied to it changes once the holding period passes one year.

Reporting is also the same. Both kinds of transactions go on Form 8949, and the totals are summarized on Schedule D of Form 1040. The form separates short-term and long-term sales into different parts, so the holding period still needs to be correct on every line.

### What Changes

The rate is the main difference. Short-term gains stack on top of wages and other ordinary income and are taxed at the same graduated rates. Long-term gains use their own thresholds. For many taxpayers in the middle of the income range, that means a lower rate on the same dollar of gain.

The order of netting also changes. Short-term results are netted against each other, and long-term results are netted against each other, before the two totals are combined. That ordering affects which gains losses offset first and how much of the remaining gain gets the lower rate.

## 2025 Long-Term Capital Gains Rate Thresholds

The IRS publishes the long-term thresholds in Topic 409. The table below uses the figures the IRS lists for taxable years beginning in 2025, which are the most recent thresholds on that page at the time of review. They are based on taxable income, not salary alone.

| Filing Status | 0% Rate Applies Up To | 15% Rate Applies Up To | 20% Rate Applies Above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | $48,350 | $533,400 | $533,400 |
| Married filing jointly | $96,700 | $600,050 | $600,050 |
| Married filing separately | $48,350 | $300,000 | $300,000 |
| Head of household | $64,750 | $566,700 | $566,700 |

### How to Read the Thresholds

The thresholds apply to taxable income, which includes the gain itself. A taxpayer near the top of the 0% band can see part of a long-term gain taxed at 0% and the rest at 15%. The rate is not a single switch that flips for the whole gain. It applies to the portion of income that falls in each band.

Thresholds also change every year for inflation. The table above is labeled 2025 because that is what the IRS page shows. Anyone estimating tax for a later year should check the IRS figures for that year before relying on any number, including the ones in this table.

### Assets With Higher Maximum Rates

Topic 409 lists three exceptions where long-term gains can be taxed above 20%. The taxable part of a gain from selling section 1202 qualified small business stock has a maximum 28% rate. Net gains from selling collectibles, such as coins or art, also have a maximum 28% rate. Unrecaptured section 1250 gain from real property has a maximum 25% rate.

These exceptions are why a generic "long-term gains are taxed at 15%" statement can mislead. A collector selling coins held for years is not in the same position as an investor selling an index fund. The type of asset is a separate question from the holding period.

## How Is the Gain Figured Before Tax?

The math starts with basis. Topic 409 says basis is generally the asset's cost to the owner, with special rules for gifts and inheritances covered in Publication 551. Adjusted basis can also include certain costs and adjustments, such as a disallowed wash sale loss added to replacement shares.

Here is a simple hypothetical. An investor buys shares for $8,000 and later sells them for $10,500, with no other costs or adjustments. The gain is $2,500. The holding period decides which rate schedule applies, but it does not change the $2,500. Many errors start when people apply a tax rate to the full sale price instead of the gain.

### Three Common Calculation Errors

The first error is treating the entire sale price as profit. Only the amount above adjusted basis is a gain. The second is applying a rate before netting losses. Losses reduce the gain first, so the rate applies to a smaller number. The third is assuming every long-term gain gets the same flat rate regardless of income or asset type.

A fourth error shows up with reinvested dividends. Each reinvestment buys shares with its own basis and its own purchase date. When an investor sells a fund held for years, some shares may be long-term and others short-term. Broker cost basis records usually split these lots, but it helps to check them.

## How Different Could the Tax Bill Look?

Here is an illustration, not a real household. Take the $2,500 gain from the example above. If the entire amount were taxed at a 24% ordinary rate as a short-term gain, the federal tax would be $600. If the same amount qualified for a 15% long-term rate, the tax would be $375. The gap in this simplified case is $225.

Real returns rarely work that cleanly. Progressive brackets mean part of a gain can fall in one band and part in another. State income taxes can add another layer, and state rules differ. The net investment income tax can apply on top. Treat the $225 figure as a picture of the mechanism, not an estimate.

### Marginal and Effective Rates

The 24% and 15% figures in the illustration are marginal rates, meaning the rate on the last dollar of income. Your effective rate is total tax divided by total income, which is usually lower. Mixing these two ideas is one of the most common sources of confusion about capital gains.

The difference counts because a capital gain is taxed at the rate for the band it falls into. If a long-term gain pushes taxable income across the 0% threshold, only the part above the threshold is taxed at 15%. The rest stays at 0%. Seeing the full picture requires knowing where your other income lands first.

### The Net Investment Income Tax

[IRS Topic 559](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc559) explains the 3.8% net investment income tax. For individuals, it applies to the lesser of net investment income or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds a threshold. The IRS lists $250,000 for married filing jointly, $125,000 for married filing separately and $200,000 for single or head of household filers.

The tax does not apply to everyone. It should not be added automatically to the capital gains rate in every estimate. For taxpayers above the thresholds, though, it can raise the top federal rate on a long-term gain above 20%. That is another reason to look at the full return rather than one transaction in isolation.

## How Do Capital Losses Change the Result?

Losses offset gains before any rate applies. Topic 409 defines net long-term capital gain as long-term gains reduced by long-term losses, including any unused long-term loss carried over from earlier years. Net short-term capital loss works the same way on the short-term side. The two totals are then combined.

If losses exceed gains for the year, Topic 409 says you can claim the lesser of $3,000 ($1,500 if married filing separately) or your total net loss against other income. Any loss above that limit carries forward to later years. The IRS points to the Capital Loss Carryover Worksheet in Publication 550 or the Schedule D instructions to figure the carryover.

### Why the Category of a Loss Counts

A short-term loss first offsets short-term gains, which are taxed at the higher ordinary rates. A long-term loss first offsets long-term gains. After that, any remaining net loss in one category can offset a net gain in the other. The order shapes how much tax a loss saves, because it decides which gains it cancels.

Investors sometimes plan sales around this order, choosing which lots to sell based on their holding period and basis. This kind of planning gets complicated quickly. A loss that is disallowed under wash sale rules does not count in the year of sale, so the transaction history across every account needs a review.

### Losses That Do Not Count

Topic 409 notes that losses from the sale of personal-use property, such as your home or car, are not tax deductible. Only losses on investment and other qualifying capital assets reduce gains. Selling a car at a loss does not offset a stock gain, even though both are capital assets.

Wash sales are the other common exception. A loss disallowed because substantially identical shares were bought within 30 days before or after the sale does not reduce gains that year. In a taxable account, the loss usually moves into the basis of the replacement shares and shows up later.

## Does Waiting to Sell Always Make Sense?

Not always. Taxes are one input to an investment decision, not the whole decision. Holding for more than a year to get long-term treatment means staying exposed to price changes for longer. A price drop larger than the tax savings would leave an investor worse off than selling earlier.

Other factors weigh in too. Cash needs, concentration in a single stock, changes in the original reason for owning an asset and transaction costs can all point in a different direction from the tax rule. FinLoom does not recommend buying, selling or holding any security. The goal here is to show how the tax rule works so it can be weighed alongside everything else.

### Timing Around Year End

The tax year also shapes the result. A sale on December 31 falls in one tax year, and a sale on January 2 falls in the next. Moving a sale across that line can change which year's income it adds to, which thresholds apply and whether losses from the same year can offset it.

Year-end timing interacts with the holding period, too. An investor close to the one-year mark in late December faces two separate timing questions at once. Writing down the purchase date, the date the holding period passes one year and the tax-year boundary makes the choice easier to see.

### Gifted and Inherited Assets

Assets you receive as a gift or inheritance follow different rules. Topic 409 says basis for these assets is covered in Publication 551, and it points to Publication 544 for how their holding periods work. The usual cost-based math and simple date count may not apply, so the records you need can come from the giver or the estate.

That is one reason two investors holding the same stock can face different results on the same sale day. ## How to Prepare a Capital Gains Estimate

A useful estimate needs a few records for each sale. Gather the purchase date, sale date, share count, proceeds, commissions or other costs, adjusted basis and any loss carryover from prior years. Then sort each sale into short-term or long-term based on the holding period.

Next, net losses against gains within each category, then combine the two. Apply the rate schedules for the correct tax year and filing status. Finally, check whether the net investment income tax or a higher maximum rate for collectibles or real estate applies. Each step builds on the last, so errors early in the list carry through.

## The Bottom Line

The short term vs long term capital gains question starts with a date, but the tax answer depends on much more. Figure the gain from adjusted basis, confirm the holding period, net the losses and only then apply the right year's rates. Labeled 2025 thresholds from the IRS give a reference point, not a guaranteed outcome for any sale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Long Must I Hold a Stock for Long-Term Treatment?

More than one year. The IRS counts from the day after you bought the asset through the day you sold it, so a sale exactly one year later is still short-term.

### Are Long-Term Capital Gains Tax-Free?

Some are. The IRS says a 0% rate applies below certain taxable income levels. Many taxpayers pay 15% or 20%, and some assets have higher maximum rates.

### Are Short-Term Gains Taxed Like Wages?

Generally yes. The IRS says net short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income at graduated rates, the same schedule that applies to wages and salary.

### How Much Capital Loss Can I Deduct Each Year?

Up to $3,000 of net capital loss, or $1,500 if married filing separately, can reduce other income each year. Any remaining loss carries forward to later years.

### Does the 3.8% Tax Apply to Everyone?

No. The net investment income tax applies only when modified adjusted gross income exceeds IRS thresholds, such as $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for joint filers.
