# HSA Investment Options: Cash Reserves, Fees, Risk and Tax Rules

A health savings account can do two jobs. It can hold cash for this year's medical bills, and at some trustees it can invest part of the balance for costs years away. Picking among HSA investment options is less about chasing the best recent return and more about balancing cash you may need soon with money you can leave alone.

That balance has real stakes. Money in the market can fall just as a large bill arrives. Flat fees can take a large bite out of a small invested balance. Tax benefits depend on how money is withdrawn. This guide uses IRS, HealthCare.gov and FDIC sources to explain the trade-offs. Sources were reviewed on November 21, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Investing depends on the trustee:** the IRS says an HSA is held by a qualified trustee, such as a bank or insurance company, and features vary.
- **Earnings grow tax free inside the account:** IRS Publication 969 says interest and other earnings in an HSA are tax free while held there.
- **Invested money is not FDIC insured:** the FDIC lists stocks, bonds and mutual funds as products it does not insure.
- **Fees vary:** HealthCare.gov notes some HSAs charge opening, closing or monthly maintenance fees.
- **Non-medical withdrawals cost more:** they are taxable and can face a 20% additional tax, except after age 65, disability or death.
- **You can move your HSA:** direct trustee-to-trustee transfers are not limited in number.

## Can HSA Money Be Invested?

Often, yes, but it depends on where the account is held. [IRS Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) describes an HSA as a tax-exempt trust or custodial account set up with a qualified HSA trustee. It says a trustee can be a bank, an insurance company or anyone already approved by the IRS to act as a trustee for IRAs or Archer MSAs.

Because trustees range from banks to firms approved for IRAs, the HSA investment options available differ widely. Some accounts work mainly like a deposit account. Others offer a list of funds or brokerage-style trading. Publication 969 also notes the HSA can be set up with a trustee that is different from your health plan provider.

### What the IRS Says About Growth

Publication 969 lists the main tax features. Interest or other earnings on the assets in the account are tax free. Contributions stay in the account until you use them. Amounts left at the end of the year generally carry over. And distributions can be tax free when they pay for qualified medical expenses.

Those features are why some people treat an HSA as a long-term account. Earnings that are not taxed each year can compound faster than earnings in a regular taxable account. The benefit only holds, though, if the rules are followed when money comes out, which is covered later in this guide.

### Deposit Balances vs Investments

The cash and investment sides of an HSA carry different protections. The FDIC says it insures deposits such as checking accounts, savings accounts and certificates of deposit up to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each insured bank. An HSA cash balance held as a bank deposit can fall under that coverage.

Investments do not. The FDIC's guide to [understanding deposit insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) lists stock investments, bond investments, mutual funds, annuities and Treasury securities among the products it does not insure. Once HSA money moves into funds, its value can rise or fall with markets.

## What to Check Before Choosing HSA Investment Options

Account rules come before fund choice. The checklist below covers the features that shape what you can invest, what it costs and how fast you can reach the money. It is a research list, not a description of any particular trustee, since features vary by provider and sometimes by employer plan.

| Feature | Question to Ask | Why It Counts |
|---|---|---|
| Cash threshold | Must a set amount stay in cash before investing? | Caps how much can be invested |
| Investment menu | Which funds or securities are offered? | Sets your diversification choices |
| Account fees | Are there opening, closing or monthly fees? | Reduces the balance over time |
| Investment fees | What platform fees and fund expense ratios apply? | Ongoing cost of investing |
| Selling and transfers | How quickly can investments be sold to pay a bill? | Affects access to cash |
| Employer arrangement | Does your employer send payroll contributions to one trustee? | Shapes convenience and cost |
| Cash yield | What interest does uninvested cash earn? | Less relevant for money left invested |

The rows group into two themes: access to cash and the cost of investing.

### Reading the Checklist

The first and fifth rows are about access. A cash threshold limits what can be invested, while selling and transfer rules decide how long it takes to turn investments back into spendable cash. Someone who pays medical bills straight from the HSA needs both rows to work in their favor.

The fee rows are about cost. HealthCare.gov's guide to [setting up an HSA](https://www.healthcare.gov/high-deductible-health-plan/setting-up-hsa/) notes that some HSAs have fees, such as charges for opening or closing the account and monthly maintenance fees. Investment platforms can add their own charges on top of fund expense ratios.

### Where to Find the Answers

Each trustee publishes a fee schedule, an investment menu and account terms. Those documents are more reliable than third-party rankings, which can repeat old fee tables. Employer benefits materials may also explain whether the employer pays some account fees or requires a specific trustee for payroll contributions.

The [best HSA accounts](/money-banking/best-hsa-accounts/) comparison reviews specific providers. Pairing a comparison like that with each trustee's current documents gives the clearest view, since features and fees can change and can differ under different employer arrangements or account tiers.

## How Much Should Stay in Cash?

There is no single right number. The amount depends on expected medical costs, other savings and comfort with market swings. Someone with steady prescription costs and regular appointments may want a larger cash cushion than someone who rarely sees a doctor. Listing known upcoming costs is a practical starting point.

Here is a hypothetical example. An account holds $6,000. The holder sets aside $2,000 in cash for expected costs. The remaining $4,000 could be invested if the trustee allows it and any cash threshold has been met. This illustrates the planning steps. It is not a recommended reserve amount, since real costs can exceed estimates.

### Thinking in Two Buckets

A simple way to frame HSA investment options is two buckets. The first bucket holds money for costs expected in the near term, kept in cash. The second holds money not expected to be needed for years, which may be invested. The split moves over time as health needs and balances change.

The buckets also connect to the plan's deductible. Someone on a high deductible health plan may face costs up to the deductible before insurance pays more. Keeping enough cash to cover that amount, or a share of it, is one way some account holders size the first bucket. The [HSA vs PPO](/money-banking/hsa-vs-ppo/) guide explains how deductibles and plan design fit together.

### Why Market Timing Counts Here

Investments can drop in the short term, even in broad, diversified funds. If a large medical bill arrives during a downturn, selling investments to pay it locks in the loss. Cash in the first bucket avoids that forced sale. That is the main reason not to invest money likely to be needed soon.

The same logic applies to saving and investing in general. Money with a near deadline usually belongs in safer, more stable places, while money with a long horizon can absorb more volatility. The [saving vs investing](/money-banking/saving-vs-investing/) guide covers that trade-off in more detail.

## How HSA Fees Add Up

Fees take two common forms: flat dollar charges and percentage charges. A flat fee costs the same whether the balance is small or large. A percentage fee, such as a fund expense ratio, scales with the balance. Comparing them separately shows how each affects accounts of different sizes.

Here is a hypothetical example. An investor has $4,000 invested, pays a $36 yearly platform fee and holds a fund with a 0.10% expense ratio. Assuming the balance stays at $4,000 for a year, the expense ratio costs $4 and the flat fee costs $36, for $40 in total. That is 1% of the invested balance.

| Invested Balance | 0.10% Expense Ratio | $36 Flat Fee | Total | Total as Share of Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $4,000 | $4 | $36 | $40 | 1.00% |
| $10,000 | $10 | $36 | $46 | 0.46% |
| $25,000 | $25 | $36 | $61 | 0.24% |

Each row uses the same two fees, so the only thing changing is the balance.

### What the Fee Table Shows

The flat fee dominates at small balances. At $4,000, it makes up 90% of the total cost in this example. At $25,000, it is closer to 59%. The percentage fee grows with the balance, but the total cost as a share of the balance falls as the account grows, because the flat fee is spread over more money.

These figures assume a constant balance and ignore market returns, trading costs and fee waivers. Some trustees waive fees above a certain balance or for certain employer plans. The real cost of any account depends on its own fee schedule, which is why reading that document is the most reliable step.

### How Fees Are Reported

Publication 969 makes one point about fees. HSA administration and maintenance fees withdrawn by the trustee are not reported as distributions from the HSA. In other words, the trustee taking its fee out of the account does not count as you taking a taxable withdrawal.

Fees still reduce the balance that can grow. Over many years, a small yearly fee compounds into a larger gap. That effect is strongest for long-term invested money, which is exactly the money people hope will grow the most. Checking the fee schedule before choosing HSA investment options helps limit that drag.

## How HSA Investments Affect Taxes

The tax benefits depend on following the rules. Publication 969 says you can claim a deduction for contributions you make even if you do not itemize, and employer contributions may be excluded from income. Earnings inside the account are tax free. Distributions used for qualified medical expenses can come out tax free.

Distributions for other reasons are different. Publication 969 says they are subject to income tax and may be subject to an additional 20% tax. It also says there is no additional tax on distributions made after the date you are disabled, reach age 65 or die. Income tax can still apply to non-medical withdrawals after 65.

### Keeping Records

Publication 969 says expenses incurred before you establish your HSA are not qualified medical expenses, and state law determines when an HSA is established. Keeping receipts for medical costs paid from the account, or paid out of pocket and reimbursed later, supports the tax-free treatment of those withdrawals.

The trustee reports distributions to you and the IRS on Form 1099-SA. Investment results do not change how a withdrawal is treated. A strong return does not turn a non-qualified withdrawal into a tax-free one, and a loss does not make a qualified one taxable.

### Federal and State Rules

The tax features described here are federal rules from Publication 969. State tax treatment of HSAs can differ, so saying every HSA investment grows tax free everywhere would overstate the case. Checking your state's rules, or asking a tax professional, fills that gap for your own situation.

The overall picture is still favorable at the federal level. Contributions can be deductible or excluded, growth is untaxed inside the account and qualified withdrawals are tax free. Few accounts combine all three, which is one reason HSA investment options attract long-term savers.

## Moving an HSA and Changing Jobs

Publication 969 says an HSA is portable and stays with you if you change employers or leave the workforce. That means your balance and any investments remain yours. It does not mean every employer will send payroll contributions to any trustee you pick.

You can move an HSA if you prefer a different trustee. Publication 969 says that if you instruct the trustee to transfer funds directly to the trustee of another of your HSAs, the transfer is not considered a rollover, and there is no limit on the number of these transfers. Such transfers are not included in income or treated as distributions.

### What a Transfer Can Involve

Moving investments between trustees can take steps. Ask both trustees whether investments must be sold to cash before a transfer, since that could leave money out of the market for a period. Others may charge a closing or transfer fee, which HealthCare.gov lists among possible HSA fees. Checking both trustees' procedures avoids surprises.

Timing also counts if you rely on the account for current bills. A transfer in progress can delay access to funds. Keeping enough cash available during a move, or timing the move for a quiet period of medical spending, helps reduce that risk.

### Rollovers Are Different

Publication 969 separates direct transfers from rollovers. A rollover contribution is not included in income, is not deductible and does not reduce your contribution limit. Rollovers follow their own rules, and Publication 969 describes those in a separate section. A direct transfer avoids several of those details.

For most people changing trustees, a direct transfer is the simpler route because it has no annual count limit. Reading the receiving trustee's transfer forms, and confirming which method they use, keeps the move within the rules.

## The Bottom Line

HSA investment options can turn an account for this year's bills into a long-term savings tool. The trade-offs come from cash needs, fees and market risk, not just fund choice. Keep near-term money in cash, check flat and percentage fees separately, remember that invested funds are not FDIC insured, and follow IRS rules on withdrawals to keep the tax benefits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an HSA Lose Money?

Yes. HSA money invested in stocks, bonds or mutual funds can fall in value. The FDIC does not insure those investments, unlike qualifying bank deposits held as cash.

### Do I Have to Invest My HSA Balance?

No. Investing is optional, and HSA investment options vary by trustee. Many account holders keep some or all of their balance in cash for medical costs.

### Is There a Minimum Before I Can Invest?

It depends on the trustee. Some require a set cash balance before investing, and others do not. Each trustee's account terms and fee schedule explain its rules.

### Are HSA Investment Gains Tax Free?

At the federal level, earnings inside an HSA are tax free, and qualified medical withdrawals are tax free. Non-medical withdrawals are taxable. State rules can differ.

### Can I Move My HSA to Another Provider?

Yes. IRS Publication 969 says direct trustee-to-trustee transfers are not rollovers and have no limit on how many you make. Check both trustees for fees.
