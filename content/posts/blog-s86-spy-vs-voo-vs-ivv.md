# SPY vs VOO vs IVV: Three S&P 500 ETFs Compared on Cost, Structure and Size

SPY, VOO and IVV all track the same benchmark, the S&P 500 Index. They come from three different issuers: State Street, Vanguard and BlackRock's iShares. Because the target is the same, the SPY vs VOO vs IVV comparison is less about what you own and more about what you pay and how each fund is built.

This guide uses the issuers' own fund pages for every figure. It lines up expense ratios, inception dates, structure and reported assets, then shows what the fee gap means in dollars with labeled examples. It does not rank past returns, because return comparisons over different windows can mislead. Sources were reviewed on November 21, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Same index:** all three issuers list the S&P 500 Index as the benchmark.
- **Two funds cost the same:** Vanguard lists VOO at 0.03% (as of April 28, 2026) and iShares lists IVV at 0.03%.
- **SPY costs more:** State Street lists a 0.0945% gross expense ratio for SPY.
- **SPY is the oldest:** SPY's inception date is January 22, 1993, compared with May 15, 2000, for IVV and September 7, 2010, for VOO.
- **SPY has a different structure:** State Street describes SPY as a unit investment trust.
- **The fee gap is small per year:** on a constant $10,000 balance, the gap between 0.0945% and 0.03% is $6.45 a year.

### Reading the Table

The benchmark row is identical, which is why holdings across the three funds are close. Each fund aims to hold the roughly 500 stocks in the index in proportion to their weight. Small differences in cash, timing of dividends and trading can still create small gaps in returns from one period to the next.

The cost row is where the funds separate. VOO and IVV list the same expense ratio. SPY lists a higher one. Because a fund's expenses are taken from its assets over time, a higher expense ratio reduces the fund's return by roughly that amount each year, all else being equal.

### Why Some Cells Say "Not Used Here"

Issuers report assets in different ways. Vanguard's page shows a total net assets figure that is not clearly limited to the ETF share class, and State Street's page did not give a single dated figure that could be matched to the other two. Rather than compare numbers measured differently, this guide only shows the IVV figure as reported.

The same caution applies to holdings counts. State Street's page showed two different holdings counts in separate sections, 503 and 504, likely reflecting different dates. Small differences in reported counts between dates are expected as the index changes.

## How the Expense Ratios Compare

According to [State Street's SPY fund page](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/individual/etfs/state-street-spdr-sp-500-etf-trust-spy), SPY's gross expense ratio is 0.0945%. State Street describes the gross expense ratio as the fund's total annual operating expense ratio, gross of any fee waivers or expense reimbursements, as found in the fund's most recent prospectus.

According to the [iShares IVV fund page](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239726/ISHARES-CORE-SP-500-ETF), IVV's expense ratio is 0.03%, labeled as fees stated in the prospectus. [Vanguard's VOO fund data](https://advisors.vanguard.com/investments/products/voo/vanguard-sp-500-etf) lists VOO's expense ratio at 0.03% as of April 28, 2026. That puts VOO and IVV level with each other and 0.0645 percentage points below SPY.

### Fees Over Longer Periods

The yearly gap is small, but it repeats every year a fund is held. Because fees reduce the balance that later grows, the effect compounds over long periods. The exact amount depends on returns, contributions and time, so a single figure cannot capture it. A calculator that models fees over time shows the effect more clearly than a one-year table.

Fees are also only one cost. ETFs carry a bid-ask spread when you buy or sell, and some brokerages charge commissions. The [ETF vs index fund](/investing/etf-vs-index-fund/) comparison explains how spreads, NAV pricing and premiums or discounts add to the total cost of owning an exchange-traded fund.

## Why SPY's Structure Is Different

State Street's page says SPY is the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, a unit investment trust. Its distributor, ALPS, is described on the same page as the distributor for SPY, MDY and DIA, all unit investment trusts. VOO and IVV are not described as unit investment trusts on their issuer pages.

The structure has practical effects that investors sometimes discuss, such as how dividends received by the fund are handled before they are paid out and what the fund is permitted to do with its holdings. This guide does not detail those rules because they were not set out on the fetched issuer pages. SPY's prospectus is the authoritative source for how the trust operates.

### SPY's Age and Options Market

SPY launched on January 22, 1993, according to State Street, which makes it the oldest of the three by more than seven years. State Street's page also lists options as available for SPY. Its long history and options availability are reasons traders often mention when they choose it over newer funds.

These features count most for active traders and options users. A buy-and-hold investor who rarely trades may weigh them less than the yearly expense ratio. The trade-off between trading features and ongoing cost is the main tension in any SPY vs VOO vs IVV decision.

### VOO and IVV Side by Side

VOO and IVV look nearly identical on the published facts long-term holders tend to check first. Both track the S&P 500. Both list a 0.03% expense ratio. IVV is older, launched May 15, 2000, according to iShares, while VOO launched September 7, 2010, according to Vanguard.

With the same index and the same fee, differences between VOO and IVV come from smaller details: tracking, cash management, dividend timing and the brokerage platform an investor uses. Some investors simply hold the fund offered by the company where they keep their account, which can simplify recordkeeping.

## Why This Guide Does Not Rank Returns

Return comparisons among the three funds are tempting but tricky. Each issuer publishes performance for different periods and as of different dates. State Street's page, for example, showed returns as of both September 30 and October 31, 2026. Mixing these, or comparing market price returns with NAV returns, can produce gaps that reflect timing, not fund quality.

Over long periods, funds tracking the same index with low fees tend to deliver returns close to the index minus fees. The fee difference is the most predictable part of the gap. Short-term differences in price, cash and dividends can move either way. FinLoom does not predict future returns or recommend any of the three funds.

### How to Compare Performance Fairly

A fair comparison uses the same start and end dates for all three funds. It uses the same return type, either NAV or market price, for each. It treats reinvested dividends the same way. The issuer fund pages and fact sheets report standardized returns that follow these conventions within each fund.

Tracking difference, the gap between a fund's return and its index's return, is often more useful than raw returns. A fund that trails its index by about its expense ratio is doing what it was designed to do. A larger or erratic gap can point to cash drag, trading costs or structural factors worth investigating.

### What Past Returns Cannot Tell You

Past performance does not guarantee future results, and that is especially true of small differences between near-identical funds. A fund that edged out the others last year may not do so next year, because the drivers are often temporary. The ongoing expense ratio is the one difference that is known in advance.

That is why many comparisons focus on cost first. An investor can see today what each fund charges. No one can see next year's tracking difference. For three funds with the same benchmark, building a decision around the known costs is more defensible than chasing a past return gap.

## SPY vs VOO vs IVV for Different Uses

In a SPY vs VOO vs IVV choice, the right fit depends on use. A long-term investor making regular purchases may focus on the expense ratio, since that cost repeats every year. A trader who buys and sells often, or who uses options, may value SPY's long history and options market even with its higher fee.

Account type and platform also play a role. Some brokerages offer commission-free trading or automatic investing in some ETFs but not others. Fractional share support varies too. These platform details can shape the practical choice as much as the fund facts do, and they change over time.

### For Buy-and-Hold Investors

For an investor who expects to hold for years, the expense ratio is the clearest differentiator. VOO and IVV list the same 0.03% fee, while SPY lists 0.0945%. On the published numbers, the two lower-fee funds cost less to hold each year. Neither is promised to outperform the other.

The detailed two-way comparison in [VOO vs SPY](/investing/voo-vs-spy/) covers how those two funds differ on dividends, structure and trading in more depth. Adding IVV to that comparison mainly confirms that VOO is not alone at the 0.03% level among large S&P 500 ETFs.

### For Traders and Options Users

Traders often weigh features that buy-and-hold investors overlook. SPY's options are listed as available on its issuer page, and its 1993 launch gives it the longest record of the three. For someone who trades frequently, the size of the spread at the moment of a trade may cost more than the yearly fee difference.

That trade-off is personal. A trader making many transactions a year experiences costs differently from someone buying monthly. Comparing the spread and the expense ratio for your own trading pattern gives a more accurate picture than any general ranking.

## How These Funds Fit a Wider Portfolio

All three funds hold large U.S. companies. They do not include small-cap stocks, international stocks or bonds. An investor holding one of them owns a single slice of the market. Choosing among SPY, VOO and IVV does not change that exposure, since all three track the same index.

The bigger portfolio decisions come before the fund choice. How much to hold in stocks versus other assets, and which assets to include at all, shape risk more than the ticker symbol. The [crypto vs stocks](/investing/crypto-vs-stocks/) guide covers one of those wider choices, comparing the risk and volatility of two very different asset classes.

### Holding More Than One of Them

Owning two or three of these funds does not add diversification, because they track the same stocks. It can add recordkeeping and, in a taxable account, more lots to track. Some investors hold different S&P 500 ETFs in different accounts for platform reasons, which is a convenience choice rather than a diversification one.

There is one tax-related consideration. Investors who sell one S&P 500 fund at a loss and buy another may face questions under the wash sale rules about whether the two are substantially identical. The IRS treats that as a facts-and-circumstances question, so it is worth reviewing before trading around a loss.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do SPY, VOO and IVV Hold the Same Stocks?

Largely yes. All three issuers list the S&P 500 Index as the benchmark, so holdings are very similar. Small differences in cash and timing can still create minor return gaps.

### Which Has the Lowest Expense Ratio?

VOO and IVV tie at 0.03% on their issuer pages, with VOO's figure dated April 28, 2026. SPY lists a 0.0945% gross expense ratio, the highest of the three.

### Why Is SPY More Expensive?

State Street lists SPY's gross expense ratio at 0.0945%. SPY is also structured as a unit investment trust, unlike the other two, and launched in 1993.

### Is It Worth Owning All Three?

Owning more than one adds little diversification because they track the same index. It can add recordkeeping, and selling one at a loss to buy another raises wash sale questions.

### How Much Does the Fee Gap Cost Each Year?

On a constant $10,000 balance, SPY's 0.0945% equals $9.45 a year and VOO's or IVV's 0.03% equals $3, a $6.45 gap. Real costs vary with your balance.
